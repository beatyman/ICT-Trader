Fair Value Gaps Explained
Reading time:
minutes
Fair value gaps (FVGs) are key to the smart money concept of FVG trading.
They show up when market sentiment shifts dramatically, causing rapid price movements on price charts.
Price action traders use these gaps to gain valuable insights into market dynamics.
The Anatomy of a Fair Value Gap
A fair value gap is typically identified by a three-candle pattern:
The first candle in the current trend direction
A strongly opposing large candle that closes beyond the open of the first candle
Third candles that continue in the direction of the second candle
Bullish FVGs appear when price moves up rapidly, while bearish fair value gaps occur during sharp price movements downward.
Visually, FVGs appear as empty spaces on candlestick charts where no trading activity has taken place, similar to traditional price gaps but forming during active trading hours.
The Psychology Behind Fair Value Gaps
FVGs represent significant market inefficiencies and supply and demand imbalances.
They often occur when large institutional trades enter or exit positions, causing rapid price movements that retail market participants cannot immediately react to.
This shift in market sentiment can lead to continued momentum in the direction of the gap, as traders adjust their positions to the new perceived intrinsic value.
Fair Value Gaps vs. Liquidity Voids
Liquidity voids are areas on a chart where there is a lack of trading activity or orders.
They can appear as large candles or spaces between price levels where the market moved quickly due to a lack of opposing orders.
While both FVGs and liquidity voids represent areas of rapid price movement, they differ in scale and formation.
FVGs are typically smaller and more frequent, often occurring on lower time frames.
Liquidity voids, on the other hand, tend to be larger and more significant, often visible on higher timeframes.
An FVG can evolve into a liquidity void if it remains unfilled for an extended period, especially when observed on higher time frames.
This transformation can have important implications for traders using multiple timeframe analysis.
Identifying Fair Value Gaps
Manual Chart Analysis
To manually identify FVGs:
Look for a series of three candles with a strong move in one direction
Check if the third candleâs open does not overlap with the first candleâs close
Confirm that the space between these levels has not been filled by subsequent price action
Be cautious of false signals, particularly in choppy market conditions where small gaps may not be significant.

Many trading platforms offer FVG indicators that automatically detect and highlight these gaps on trading charts.

The âGap Fillâ Strategy
The gap fill strategy is based on the principle that price often returns to âfillâ the FVG. Traders can:
Enter trades when price approaches a previously identified FVG
Set profit targets at the opposite end of the gap
Place stop-loss orders beyond the gapâs furthest point
Risk management is crucial, as not all gaps will fill completely or immediately.
Trend Continuation Trades
FVGs can be used to confirm market trends:
Identify the overall trend direction (bullish trend or bearish trend)
Look for FVGs that form in the direction of the trend
Enter trades when price retraces to the FVG level
Combining FVGs with other technical analysis tools, such as trend lines or moving averages, can increase the strategyâs robustness.

Timing entries is critical in reversal trading to avoid premature positions against a strong trend.

FVGs often interact with other price action concepts:

Breaks of structure: FVGs can form during significant market structure shifts
Change of character: FVGs may signal a change when they form against the prevailing trend
Liquidity grabs: Institutional investors may use FVGs to trap retail traders before major moves

Higher timeframe context is crucial when trading FVGs:

Use lower timeframe FVGs for precise entry and exit points
Ensure alignment between FVGs across different timeframes for stronger signals
Fair Value Gaps in Different Markets
FVGs occur in various financial markets, but their behavior may differ:
Forex market: FVGs are common due to 24-hour trading and high liquidity
Stocks: FVGs often form around earnings reports or significant new information
Commodities: FVGs may be influenced by supply and demand shocks
Cryptocurrencies: High market volatility can create numerous FVGs, requiring careful filtering
Market Manipulation and FVGs
Institutional investors may use FVGs to trap retail traders:

Using a fixed risk percentage based on account size
Consider using adjacent structure levels to refine stop loss decisions.
Calculate appropriate position sizes based on:
The distance between entry and stop loss
Account risk tolerance (typically 1-2% per trade)
Gap magnitude (larger gaps may warrant smaller positions due to increased market volatility)
Backtesting and Optimizing FVG Strategies
Key components of an FVG-based system:

Limitations and Criticisms of Fair Value Gap Trading
FVGs may not always be filled due to:
Changing market conditions
Strong trend continuation
Fundamental shifts in intrinsic value
Market volatility or low liquidity can affect FVG reliability.
Basing trades solely on FVGs can be risky:
Ignoring other important technical and fundamental factors
Missing broader market context
Increased vulnerability to false signals
A comprehensive approach incorporating multiple analysis methods is generally more robust.

Steps to incorporate FVGs:

Risk Reward Ratio 101
Reading time:
2
minutes
The risk-reward ratio is a trader's best friend.
It's a simple tool that compares potential reward to potential risk.
It helps you make investment decisions and determine if a trade is worth your time and money. It's like a financial scale, balancing risk on one side and reward on the other.
In the fast-paced world of trading and investing, making informed decisions is crucial.
The risk-reward ratio provides a quantitative approach to evaluating trades, helping investors manage their capital more effectively.
By understanding and applying this concept, traders can improve their chances of long-term success in the financial markets.
Fundamentals of Risk and Reward
A. What is risk in financial markets?
Risk is the chance you might lose money. It includes:
Market risk:
The market might tank.
Credit risk:
Someone might not pay you back.
Liquidity risk:
You can't sell when you want to.
Operational risk:
Something goes wrong behind the scenes.
We measure risk using math like standard deviation.
You don't need to be a math expert to use risk-reward ratios. What matters is knowing your risk tolerance and the amount of risk you're comfortable with.
Risk is an inherent part of investing.
Without risk, there would be no potential for reward.
The key is to understand the different types of risk and how they apply to your investments.
For example, market risk affects all investments to some degree, while credit risk is more specific to bonds and other debt instruments.
B. What is reward in financial markets?
Reward is the money you hope to make.
It could be:
Capital gains:
Your asset becomes more valuable.
Income:
You get paid dividends or interest.
Predicting rewards isn't easy.
It's part science, part educated guessing. Your expected return depends on market conditions and your trading strategy.
The potential reward of an investment is what attracts investors in the first place.
However, it's important to remember that higher potential rewards often come with higher risks. This is where the risk-reward ratio becomes particularly useful, helping investors balance their desire for returns with their need for safety.
The Risk-Reward Ratio Explained
The risk-reward ratio is simple math.
Divide potential gain by potential loss.
A 1:3 ratio means for every unit of risk taken, you could make three units of profit.
Higher ratios are often better, but there's more to consider. A lower ratio might work if you have a high win rate.
For example, if you're risking $100 on a trade with a potential profit of $300, your risk-reward ratio is 1:3.
This means you're risking one unit ($100) to potentially gain three units ($300).
It's important to note that the risk-reward ratio doesn't tell you the likelihood of success.
It merely quantifies the relationship between the potential risk and reward.
A trade with a favorable risk-reward ratio can still be a poor choice if the probability of success is low.
Calculating the Risk-Reward Ratio
Here's how to calculate it:
Pick your entry point
Set a stop-loss order
Choose a profit target
Do the math
Let's say you're buying a
head and shoulders pattern
breakout at $100. You'll sell if it drops to $95 (stop-loss) or rises to $115 (take profit level). Your risk is $5, your potential reward is $15. That's a 1:3 ratio. This calculation is important for determining a trade's potential profitability.
To calculate the ratio:
Risk = Entry price - Stop loss price = $100 - $95 = $5
Reward = Take profit price - Entry price = $115 - $100 = $15
Risk-Reward Ratio = Reward / Risk = $15 / $5 = 3:1
This ratio tells you that for every dollar you risk, you stand to gain three dollars if the trade goes in your favor.
Importance of Risk-Reward Ratio in Trading
This ratio is vital for risk management.
It helps you pick smart trades and stay disciplined.
Even if you lose more often than you win, a good ratio can keep you profitable.
It's especially important in volatile markets like forex or when dealing with currency pairs.
The risk-reward ratio helps traders in several ways:
Trade Selection:
It helps identify trades with the best potential return for the risk involved.
Position Sizing:
Knowing the risk-reward ratio can help determine how much capital to allocate to each trade.
Emotional Control:
Having a predetermined ratio can help traders stick to their strategy and avoid emotional decisions.
Long-term Profitability:
Even with a win rate below 50%, traders can be profitable if they maintain a favorable risk-reward ratio.
For example, if a trader wins 40% of their trades but maintains a 1:3 risk-reward ratio, they can still be profitable:
60 losing trades:
60 * -1 unit = -60 units
40 winning trades:
40 * 3 units = 120 units
Net result:
120 - 60 = 60 units profit
Risk-Reward Profiles
Traders come in three types:
Risk-averse:
Play it safe, prefer lower risk
Risk-neutral:
Balanced approach
Risk-seeking:
High risk, high reward
Know yourself. Your risk appetite should match your trading style.
Conservative investors might prefer a higher reward-to-risk ratio, while others might accept more risk for higher potential returns.
Understanding your risk profile is crucial for developing a sustainable trading strategy.
A risk-averse trader might look for trades with a risk-reward ratio of 1:3 or higher, while a risk-seeking trader might be comfortable with a 1:1.5 ratio if they believe they have a high probability of success.
It's also important to note that your risk profile may change over time.
Factors such as age, financial situation, and market conditions can all influence your risk tolerance.
Regularly reassessing your risk profile can help ensure your trading strategy remains aligned with your goals and comfort level.
Advanced Concepts
The risk-reward ratio isn't just for individual trades.
Use it for position sizing and portfolio management too.
It applies to various financial instruments, from stocks to mutual funds.
Advanced applications of the risk-reward concept include:
Portfolio Risk Management:
Apply the concept to your entire portfolio, balancing high-risk and low-risk investments.
Risk-Adjusted Returns:
Use metrics like the Sharpe ratio to evaluate investments based on their return relative to their risk.
Options Strategies:
In options trading, the risk-reward ratio can help in selecting appropriate strategies for different market conditions.
Pairs Trading:
When trading correlated pairs of securities, the risk-reward ratio can help identify optimal entry and exit points.
Remember, while the risk-reward ratio is a powerful tool, it should be used in conjunction with other analysis techniques for best results.
Common Mistakes and Pitfalls
Avoid these traps:
Ignoring win probability
Relying solely on the ratio
Forgetting about market volatility
Abandoning your plan mid-trade
Remember, past performance doesn't guarantee future results. Always consider your financial situation and current market conditions before trading.
One common mistake is focusing too much on high reward-to-risk ratios without considering the probability of success.
A trade with a 1:10 ratio might seem attractive, but if the chances of success are very low, it may not be a good trade.
Another pitfall is not adjusting the risk-reward ratio for different market conditions.
In highly volatile markets, you might need to widen your stop-loss levels, which could affect your ratio.
Always be prepared to adapt your strategy to changing market conditions.
Risk-Reward Ratio in Different Trading Styles
Different approaches for different traders:
Day traders:
Quick trades, tighter ratios such as bearish
candlestick patterns
.
Swing traders:
Wider ratios for bigger moves and
trading patterns
.
Position traders:
Even wider ratios for long-term success
Scalpers:
Tiny profits, very tight ratios
Your trading account balance and margin requirements might influence which style suits you best.
Day traders often work with tighter risk-reward ratios due to the short-term nature of their trades.
They might aim for ratios like 1:1.5 or 1:2, making up for the lower reward with a higher frequency of trades.
Swing traders, who hold positions for days or weeks, might look for ratios of 1:3 or higher.
They have more time for trades to develop, allowing for wider stop-loss levels and higher profit targets.
Position traders, focusing on long-term trends, might seek even higher ratios, sometimes 1:5 or more.
Their longer time horizon allows them to weather short-term fluctuations in pursuit of larger gains.
Scalpers, who aim to profit from very small price movements, often use tight ratios like 1:1 or even less.
They compensate for the low reward-to-risk ratio with a very high win rate and a large number of trades.

trading journal templates

Risk Management Strategies Beyond Risk-Reward Ratio
The ratio is just one tool.
Also consider:
Diversification:
Spread your investments
Hedging:
Insurance for your trades
Smart leverage:
Borrowed money can amplify gains (and losses)
Regular rebalancing:
Keep your portfolio on track
These strategies can help you manage your account balance and achieve long-term success.
Diversification involves spreading your investments across different asset classes, sectors, and geographical regions. This can help reduce the impact of poor performance in any single investment.
Hedging is a strategy used to offset potential losses in one investment by taking an opposite position in a related investment. For example, an investor holding a stock might buy a put option on that stock to protect against potential downside.
Leverage can amplify both gains and losses, so it should be used cautiously. While it can increase potential returns, it also increases risk. Always understand the terms of leveraged trades and never risk more than you can afford to lose.
Regular rebalancing involves periodically adjusting your portfolio back to your target asset allocation.
This helps maintain your desired level of risk and can improve long-term returns by systematically "buying low and selling high."
Case Studies
Examples help explain this concept. A well-timed trend reversal with a 1:5 ratio can be a big win. But be careful of attractive ratios with low win probabilities. Technical analysis can help identify good entry and exit points for profitable trades.
Case Study 1: Forex Trading
A forex trader spots a potential trend reversal in the EUR/USD pair. They enter a long position at 1.1000, setting a stop-loss at 1.0950 and a take-profit at 1.1250. This creates a risk-reward ratio of 1:5 (50 pips risk for 250 pips reward). The trade succeeds, resulting in a significant profit despite the trader's overall win rate being only 40%.
Case Study 2: Stock Trading
A stock trader identifies a bullish pattern in a stock trading at $50. They set a stop-loss at $48 and a take-profit at $56, creating a 1:3 risk-reward ratio. However, after entering the trade, they get nervous and exit early at $52, effectively changing their ratio to 1:1. This highlights the importance of sticking to your predetermined plan.
Case Study 3: Options Trading
An options trader sees a potential for a small upward move in a stock. They buy a call option with a risk-reward ratio of only 1:1.5, but their analysis suggests a 70% chance of success. Despite the lower ratio, the high probability makes this an attractive trade.
These case studies illustrate how the risk-reward ratio interacts with other factors like win rate, trader psychology, and market analysis to determine the overall success of a trading strategy.
Risk-Reward Ratio in Different Markets
The concept applies everywhere, but details vary:
Stock market:
Often wider ratios based on share price movements
Forex market:
Tighter ratios due to leverage
Futures:
Varies widely
Options:
Complex strategies need careful calculation
Each market has its own characteristics and basic trading terms you should understand.
In the
stock market
, traders often use wider risk-reward ratios due to the potential for significant price movements over time. A ratio of 1:3 or higher is common, especially for swing or position traders.
The
forex market
, known for its high liquidity and use of leverage, often sees traders using tighter ratios. Day traders in forex might use ratios as low as 1:1.5 or 1:2, making up for the lower reward with more frequent trading opportunities.
Futures markets
can vary widely depending on the specific contract and market conditions. Commodities futures, for instance, can be highly volatile, potentially allowing for wider risk-reward ratios.
Options trading
introduces additional complexity due to factors like time decay and implied volatility. Traders might use different ratios for different strategies. For example, a credit spread might aim for a lower reward-to-risk ratio but with a higher probability of success.
Regulatory Considerations
Regulators want transparency. Brokers and advisors must explain risks and rewards to clients. Always read the privacy policy and related documents when opening brokerage accounts.
Financial regulators around the world have implemented rules to protect investors and maintain market integrity. In the United States, for example, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) oversee various aspects of trading and investing.
These regulations often require brokers and financial advisors to:
Provide clear information about the risks associated with different types of investments
Disclose potential conflicts of interest
Ensure that investment recommendations are suitable for the client's financial situation and goals
Maintain accurate records of trades and client communications
When using the risk-reward ratio in your trading, it's important to understand how these regulations might affect your strategy. For example, some jurisdictions have restrictions on leverage or the types of products that can be offered to retail investors.

The future is tech-driven:
AI for smarter risk assessment
Blockchain integration for decentralized risk management
New models for complex, connected markets
Keep an eye on crypto guides and new financial products as they may offer opportunities with different risk-reward profiles.

The rise of cryptocurrencies and decentralized finance (DeFi) is introducing new asset classes with unique risk-reward characteristics. Traders will need to adapt their strategies to these new markets, which often exhibit high volatility and novel risks.
Conclusion
The risk-reward ratio is a powerful tool, but it's not magic. Use it wisely, with other strategies. Successful trading is about managing risk, not just chasing rewards.
Stay informed and disciplined: whether you're dealing with a savings account or high-risk trades, understanding this concept is useful for all individual investors.
Remember, the risk-reward ratio is just one piece of the puzzle. It should be used in conjunction with thorough market analysis, a solid understanding of the assets you're trading, and a well-defined overall trading strategy. By combining these elements, you can work towards becoming a more effective and profitable trader.
As you continue your trading journey, regularly review and adjust your approach to risk and reward. Markets change, and so should your strategies. Stay curious, keep learning, and always prioritize risk management in your trading activities. With patience and discipline, you can use the risk-reward ratio as a valuable tool in your path to trading success.

How to Trade Supply and Demand
Reading time:
minutes
Among the financial markets, supply and demand trading stands as a very powerful tool; it not only operates in the Forex market but also in the security market.
This guide will help you understand and implement the technique correctly with a major focal point on how to identify and use supply and demand zones for better trading decisions.
Supply and demand trading is the application of the ancient laws of supply and demand to the trading of financial instruments.
It focuses on identifying those areas where the price is more likely to react to an imbalance between buy orders and sell orders.
In trading, these areas are called:
Supply zones: Where selling pressure can be anticipated
Demand zones: Where buying pressure can be anticipated
It gives a way of understanding market structure and determining the price movement in potential form.
Traders, upon recognizing these zones, make more informed trading decisions on when to enter or exit trades.
The supply and demand trading concept is based on how markets move in cycles of accumulation versus distribution.
Institutional traders, smart money, buy during times of accumulation, creating demand areas. Distribution is when these players sell and create supply zones.
How to Understand Supply and Demand Zones
Supply and Demand Zones Explained
The supply zones are those areas of the chart where selling pressure is expected to push prices lower.
They usually form after a big drop in price and its subsequent rally.
When the price comes back to this area, sellers who missed the initial drop may enter short positions against it, creating resistance levels.
Demand zones are places where buying pressure is expected to push prices higher.
The areas are formed after the price rises and their consequent drops.
When the price comes back to this area, then buyers who missed the initial rise may enter long positions, creating support levels.
How They Differ from Support and Resistance Levels
Undoubtedly, supply and demand zones share similarities with support and resistance, but they are different in the following ways:
Dynamic nature: Supply and demand zones behave more like liquid and can, therefore, be dynamic in nature.
Consideration of Context: They consider market conditions and order flow.
Variation in Strength: Their strength differs through variables such as volume and time frame.
Traditional support and resistance levels are normally static and based on historical price points; the supply and demand zones, on the other hand, take into consideration the underlying market dynamics.
The Psychology Behind the Zones
The market participants, especially retail traders, remember certain price levels at which enormous buying or selling took place.
In such scenarios, this type of collective memory can trigger strong responses when the price returns to these zones.
For instance, if one missing trader had missed a big move, he would wait for the price to return to a certain level before entering, hence creating a self-fulfilling prophecy.
Identifying Supply and Demand Zones
Key Characteristics of Strong Zones
Sharp price rejections: The price often moves decisively away from the zone in strong zones.
High volume: If significant trading activity occurred during the formation of the zone, that would indicate an increased importance.
Fast formation: Zones that form over a very short period are often stronger than ones that develop over a long period.
Types of Zones
Baseline zones: These types of zones form during price consolidation, which might indicate the potential for breakouts.
Drop-base-drop and rally-base-rally: These occur during strong upward or downward trends, indicating continuation patterns.
Rally-base-drop and drop-base-rally: These may indicate impending market reversals and thus find an application in trading against the trend.
Identification Tools
Use price action analysis through volume indicators and technical indicators, such as RSI and MACD, to locate such potential zones.
Price action patterns like pin bars â better known as the
hammer candlestick
when bullish â or engulfing candles near these zones will provide extra confirmation.
For example, if there is a bullish pin bar occurring right at a demand zone, then it could be a buying opportunity.
On the other hand, a bearish engulfing pattern at a supply zone might indicate a selling opportunity.
Four Main Patterns of Supply and Demand Trading
Rally-Base-Rally (RBR): Up-trend, consolidation, and another up-move. This formation further indicates that the bullishness is not ended yet.
DBD: Drop-Base-Drop. A downward movement, then some consolidation, followed by another downward move. An indication of more sustained bearish pressures.
RBD: Rally-Base-Drop. An upward move, then some consolidation, and finally a downward movement. It could be signaling a possible trend reversal from bullish to bearish.
DBR: Drop-Base-Rally. A downward trend, followed by some consolidation, then an upward move. It might indicate a shift from bearish to bullish sentiment.
Each pattern presents specific trading opportunities.
For example, in an RBR pattern, the trader would normally look for buying positions at the base of the pattern and expect that the rally would further climb.
Advanced Concepts in Trading with Supply and Demand
Fresh zones versus tested zones:
Fresh zones which price hasnât visited again are stronger compared with those tested zones.
Zone strength:
A zone can be considered weak, average, or strong, taking into account several other factors, like the speed of price rejection or volume in its formation.
Zone confluence:
If the zone formed results from the confluence of other technical factorsâsuch as pivot points, Fibonacci levels, or trend linesâthe strength is greater.
Multitimeframe analysis:
The scanning of the zones in higher time frames can add to the reliability of trading decisions. For example, if there is a demand zone on the daily chart that is combined with the similar zone on the 4-hour chart, it will be considered stronger.
Trading Strategies in Supply and Demand
Reversal Trading Strategy:
Entry: When the price enters into a strong supply or demand zone.
Stop Loss: Below demand zone for longs, above supply zone for shorts
Take Profit: At the next significant supply or demand zone
Breakout Trading Strategy:
Entry: Price is moving through a zone with momentum
Stop Loss: Just inside the broken zone
Take Profit: Next significant supply or demand zone
Trend Continuation Strategy:
Entry: On pullbacks to zones in the trend direction
Stop Loss: Beyond the zone in the opposite direction of the trend
Take Profit: At the next trend-aligned supply or demand zone
Range Trading Strategy:
Entry: At established supply and demand zones within a ranging market
Stop Loss: Beyond the respective supply or demand zone
Take Profit: At the opposite zone of the range
With each strategy, setting an entry criteria, proper stop loss, and take profit levels should be done based on how much you can tolerate the risk and your market analysis.
Supply and Demand Risk Management in Trading
One of the main factors for long-term trading success is proper risk management.
Put a 1-2% risk of trading capital per trade to decrease the chance of a significantly large drawdown on your account.
Always use stops; place them at a reasonable distance from a supply or demand zone to provide room for fluctuation in price.
Utilize both fixed take-profit levels and trailing stops in a combined fashion to maximize your gain, yet protect profit.
Be ready to exit quickly in case of a failed breakout. This is common in trading.
Ensure a minimum risk-reward ratio of 1:2. In other words, the potential profit should at least be double the potential loss taken on each trade.
Integrating Supply and Demand into Other Techniques
Integrating supply and demand analysis with other techniques in trading will come out with a much stronger trading signal. This is so because through it, one is able to:
Trend analysis through trend lines: Identify the overall market direction and trade in line with the trend.
Candlestick patterns: Setzetting candlestick patterns at supply and demand zones.
Fibonacci levels: Many times, they will match up with supply and demand zones to add confluence.
Order flow analysis: Knowing order flow can help in being proactive about potential reactions at supply and demand zones.
For example, if there is one demand zone that converges at a 61.8% Fibonacci retracement in an uptrend, further validated by a candlestick formation called the bullish engulfing, that can create a high-probability trading opportunity.
Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them
Overtrading minor zones: Stick to only trading at the most prominent and well-defined zones to minimize overtrading.
Ignoring the bigger picture of market trend and economic conditions.
Unable to adjust to the change of market movement: Always be ready to change your strategy whenever the market dynamics change.
Poor risk management: Religious adherence to the risk management principles will go a long way in preserving trading capital.
Advanced Tips on Trading Supply and Demand
Higher time frames give more reliable zones.: Generally, the higher the time frame, the greater the zones are.
Keep in mind the basic factors that could impact supply and demand: News items and other events can make a zone strong.

The symmetrical triangle is a continuation pattern, but unlike the ascending and descending triangles, it has a different shape. As for trading volume, it will fall along with the price until both reach the bottom of the cup, after which they will rise again.

Day traders use the head and shoulders pattern to identify trend reversals.
