On-Neck and In-Neck Continuation Patterns: Complete Guide
Ben Losier
October 27, 2026
Price Action Strategies
On-neck and in-neck continuation patterns are two-candle bearish formations that signal the resumption of an established downtrend following a weak, rejected counter-trend rally. These patterns alert short sellers that buyers lack the momentum required to pierce overhead resistance, making them essential tools for trend traders looking to enter high-probability short positions. This definitive guide details the structural mechanics, exact trade parameters, and execution rules needed to trade both setups with precision.
On-neck and in-neck patterns are strictly bearish continuation formations occurring exclusively within established downtrends.
The on-neck pattern closes precisely at or slightly below the previous candle’s low, while the in-neck pattern closes marginally inside the prior red candle’s real body.
Both patterns reflect failed buyer absorption, confirming that bears retain decisive control of the order flow.
Trading confirmation requires a breakdown below the low of the second candle rather than entering blindly on the close.
These setups deliver their highest win rates on daily and 4-hour charts when paired with declining volume on the retracement.
What Are On-Neck and In-Neck Continuation Patterns?
On-neck and in-neck continuation patterns are two-bar bearish price formations characterized by a long downward candle followed by a smaller upward candle that fails to penetrate the prior decline.
First documented systematically by Steve Nison in his foundational work on Japanese candlestick analysis, these patterns originally emerged in the rice markets to identify periods where counter-trend buying pressure stalled out immediately upon contact with supply. The patterns appear almost exclusively within downward market structures across equities, forex, futures, and commodities. Traders track these patterns because they offer clean visual evidence of buyer exhaustion without requiring secondary lagging indicators. Instead of signaling a reversal, they confirm that the prevailing path of least resistance remains decisively downward.

The Market Psychology Behind On-Neck and In-Neck Patterns
The market psychology driving on-neck and in-neck patterns reveals aggressive dip-buying being completely overwhelmed and trapped by dominant institutional supply.
On the first bar, institutional sellers drive price downward with authority, printing a tall, wide-range red candle that sweeps liquidity and establishes a fresh swing low. The subsequent session opens with a downward gap, driven by lingering panic or overnight short momentum. Value-seeking buyers and profit-taking shorts step in to lift the market, creating an intraday rally. However, this counter-move is shallow and weak; buyers fail to muster enough volume to push price substantially into the prior candle’s territory. When the session closes right against the prior low (on-neck) or barely penetrating the real body (in-neck), the buyers realize their trapped position, triggering a cascade of protective stop orders that fuels the next leg down.
How to identify the pattern on a chart
How to Identify the On-Neck and In-Neck Patterns on a Chart
Accurate identification requires verifying a clear preceding downtrend, strict body-to-wick proportions, and precise closing price relationships between the two candles.
Look for these structural details across both bars.
Candle One: Dominant Bearish Expansion
The first candle must be a decisive bearish candle with an above-average real body, reflecting strong selling conviction. It should close near its low with minimal lower shadow, demonstrating that sellers controlled the session right into the close.
The Downside Gap
Between candle one and candle two, there must be a visible open gap to the downside on daily charts, or an immediate lower open on intraday charts. This gap illustrates that selling pressure carried through the inter-session period.
Candle Two: The Exhausted Retracement
The second candle must be a small-bodied bullish (or occasionally neutral) candle. Its real body must be notably smaller than the first candle—typically less than one-third the height of the first candle’s body. The upper wick must be short, indicating that buyers could not sustain any higher intraday push.
The Critical Closing Boundary
The critical dividing line between the two variations lies in the closing print of the second candle. For an on-neck pattern, the second candle must close exactly at, or a fraction of a tick below, the previous candle’s low price. For an in-neck pattern, the close pushes marginally above the prior candle’s low, finishing slightly inside the lower extremity of the first candle’s real body, but never exceeding the lower 10% of that body.
The Exact Setup Criteria for On-Neck and In-Neck Formations
To eliminate false continuation signals, enter only when the market satisfies every condition in this setup checklist.
The market must reside in a clear downtrend, confirmed by price trading below the downward-sloping 20-period and 50-period Exponential
Moving Averages
(EMA).
Candle one must be a large red candle whose real body exceeds 1.5 times the 14-period
Average True Range
(ATR).
Candle two must open below the low of candle one.
Candle two must close either at the low of candle one (on-neck) or penetrate no more than 10% into candle one’s real body (in-neck).
Volume on candle one must be elevated above the 20-period volume average, while volume on candle two must contract, reflecting a lack of buying commitment.
A trigger session must break and close beneath the low of candle two within the next two candles to validate execution.
Trade setup: entry, stop loss, and profit target
How Do You Trade the On-Neck and In-Neck Patterns? (Entry, Stop Loss, Target)
Trade on-neck and in-neck patterns by executing a sell-stop order placed one tick below the low of the second candle, anchoring your stop loss above its high.
Never short blindly at the market the moment the second candle closes. Wait for price to confirm continuation by breaking the pattern’s floor.
Place your entry sell-stop order 1 to 2 ticks below the absolute low of candle two. If price turns and rallies above candle two without triggering your entry, cancel the order immediately. Anchor your protective stop loss 1 ATR above the high of candle two; for tighter, more aggressive setups, place the stop loss 1 tick above the midpoint of candle one’s real body. Target profits using a fixed 2:1 or 3:1 Reward-to-Risk ratio, or anchor your profit target at the next major daily support level or liquidity pool beneath the market.

risk management
rules operate in real conditions.
As you can see in the annotated chart above, stock XYZ is locked in a distinct daily downtrend, printing consecutive lower highs and lower lows beneath a declining 20 EMA. On Tuesday, XYZ trades at $52.00 before aggressive institutional selling sends shares tumbling to a close of $48.50, printing a massive red candle with a 14-period ATR reading of $1.20. The low of this session is $48.40.
On Wednesday, XYZ opens lower at $47.80, gapping down 60 cents. Intraday bargain hunters step in, pushing the stock back up to an intraday high of $48.60. By the closing bell, however, the rally stalls out completely, and the candle prints a small green body that closes at exactly $48.40—precisely matching Tuesday’s low. This confirms a textbook on-neck continuation pattern. Wednesday’s volume drops 45% compared to Tuesday’s volume surge.
You set a sell-stop order at $47.75 (5 cents below Wednesday’s low of $47.80). You calculate your stop loss at $49.20 (1 ATR, or $1.20, above Wednesday’s high of $48.00, rounded to allow for volatility). This creates a per-share risk of $1.45 ($49.20 – $47.75). On Thursday morning, XYZ breaks below $47.75 within the first 30 minutes, triggering your short position. You set your take-profit target at $43.40, securing a 3:1 reward-to-risk ratio ($4.35 profit vs $1.45 risk). Over the following four sessions, trapped buyers liquidate, and XYZ hits the $43.40 target cleanly.

On-Neck and In-Neck Across Different Timeframes
Daily and 4-hour timeframes produce the cleanest and most dependable signals for on-neck and in-neck patterns due to lower market noise and true structural session gaps.
5-Minute and 15-Minute Charts:
Intraday timeframes suffer from high market noise, spread distortions, and frequent micro-reversals. True gaps rarely occur except at the session cash open, making exact neck-line closes unreliable. Avoid trading this pattern as a standalone signal below the 1-hour chart.
1-Hour and 4-Hour Charts:
Highly effective for swing traders in forex and liquid futures. The gaps manifest primarily at session handoffs (such as London into New York), and the volume drop-offs on the counter-trend candle accurately reflect real institutional absence.
Daily Charts:
The gold standard for on-neck and in-neck patterns. True inter-day opening gaps reveal real overnight sentiment, and institutional order books respect the prior day’s close and low levels with extreme precision.
Weekly Charts:
Rare, but exceptionally powerful when they appear. A weekly on-neck pattern marks multi-month macro distribution phases that often produce extended, sustained downward trends.
On-Neck Pattern vs. Piercing Line: Key Differences
The on-neck pattern is a bearish continuation setup, whereas the piercing line is a bullish reversal pattern, separated entirely by the closing depth of the second candle.
The On-Neck Pattern
The on-neck pattern begins with a large bearish candle followed by a gap-down open. The second candle stages a weak recovery that closes strictly at or slightly below the first candle’s low. It confirms that the bears completely shut down the counter-attack, signaling that the ongoing downtrend is set to accelerate.
The Piercing Line Pattern
The piercing line also begins with a large bearish candle and opens with a downside gap. However, the subsequent buying wave is aggressive, driving the second candle to close well above the 50% midpoint of the first candle’s real body. This deep penetration into bearish territory shatters seller dominance and indicates a strong, high-probability trend reversal.
Best Confluences to Stack With On-Neck and In-Neck Patterns
Layering objective technical confluences on top of the two-candle anatomy drastically elevates trade expectancy.
20-period and 50-period EMAs:
Look for the on-neck or in-neck pattern to form as a dynamic pullback directly into a declining 20 or 50 EMA. When the small green candle taps the moving average and rejects it, dynamic trend resistance confirms the setup.
Prior Support Turned Resistance:
The pattern carries maximum authority when the low of candle one aligns horizontally with a broken prior swing low. This turns former support into a supply ceiling that caps candle two.
Volume
Divergence
:
Strong volume on the downward expansion candle followed by drying volume on the retracement candle confirms that major players are not participating in the bounce.
VWAP
(Volume-Weighted Average Price):
In intraday and short-term swing contexts, an on-neck pattern closing below a descending session or weekly VWAP shows that institutions remain net sellers at the average transacted price.
RSI
Bearish Regime (Below 50):
Ensure the Relative Strength Index is oscillating between 30 and 50. An RSI reading pinned below its midline confirms dominant bearish momentum without yet reaching oversold territory.
Common On-Neck and In-Neck Mistakes to Avoid
Traders consistently lose capital on these patterns by misreading candle structure, forcing trades in sideways markets, and rushing execution.
Entering on the close of the second candle:
Buying pressure may still have intraday momentum; jumping in before price breaks candle two’s low frequently results in being stopped out by extended pullbacks.
Trading inside consolidation ranges:
These patterns require strong momentum behind them. In range-bound markets, small green candles closing near prior lows are simply chop, not continuation patterns.
Confusing an in-neck with a meeting lines pattern:
A bearish meeting lines pattern closes at the close of candle one, whereas an in-neck pattern closes slightly inside its body. Misidentifying the closing tier distorts your risk profile.
Ignoring an oversold macro environment:
Taking an on-neck continuation setup directly into a multi-year horizontal support level or extreme daily oversold conditions often leads to sudden short squeezes.
Placing stops too close to the trigger price:
Setting stops just above the tiny body of candle two leaves positions vulnerable to routine spread widening and noise; always anchor stops outside the structure with an ATR buffer.

The Complete Guide to ICT Fair Value Gaps in Forex
Ben Losier
October 26, 2026
ICT vs SMC
An ICT
Fair Value Gap
(FVG) is a three-candle price pattern where aggressive institutional order flow creates a sudden imbalance between buyers and sellers, leaving an unfilled price void between the first candle’s wick and the third candle’s wick. When smart money players execute massive market orders, they exhaust opposing liquidity, leaving resting orders behind that price must later revisit to rebalance the market auction. For forex traders seeking high-probability trade locations, the ICT Fair Value Gap provides an exact, objective entry model that eliminates guesswork by revealing institutional sponsorship directly on raw candlestick charts.

What Is ICT Fair Value Gap?
An ICT Fair Value Gap is an imbalance in price delivery created when the market moves rapidly in one direction across a three-candle sequence, leaving one side of the auction entirely unoffered. Developed by Michael J. Huddleston, the founder of Inner Circle Trader (ICT), this pattern reflects institutional displacement where price covers significant ground without two-sided trade execution. In modern
Smart Money Concepts
(SMC), this structure is also known as a price imbalance, liquidity void, or single-print zone.

An ICT Fair Value Gap exists in two distinct forms: a Bullish FVG, technically classified as a Buy-Side Imbalance Sell-Side Inefficiency (BISI), and a Bearish FVG, classified as a Sell-Side Imbalance Buy-Side Inefficiency (SIBI). In a bullish gap, buyers dominate the market so aggressively that sellers never have the opportunity to transact between Candle 1’s high and Candle 3’s low. Conversely, a bearish gap develops when heavy selling leaves buy orders completely unmatched between Candle 1’s low and Candle 3’s high. The resulting gap acts like a market vacuum, pulling price back into its boundaries to balance the delivery of price before continuing in the direction of the dominant institutional flow.
Why This Edge Works
The edge behind the ICT Fair Value Gap lies in the core mechanics of algorithmic trade routing and liquidity provisioning. Retail traders are often taught that price moves smoothly across
supply and demand
curves, but interbank algorithms operate on delivery efficiency. When major central banks, commercial institutions, and large market participants transact, their position size overwhelms the available limit orders at existing price increments. This creates rapid market displacement, leaving resting buy or sell limit orders unfilled at lower and higher price levels.
Because market makers earn fees by facilitating balanced, two-sided volume, the Interbank Price Delivery Algorithm (IPDA) systematically re-auctions price back into these low-volume pockets. This process is called price rebalancing or mitigation. When price revisits an ICT Fair Value Gap, it fills the missing liquidity on the underrepresented side of the order book. Once this imbalance is resolved, the market no longer has an auction reason to remain at that price, triggering a fast, explosive continuation back toward the primary draw on liquidity.
When I trade this pattern, I never view an FVG as a mere support or resistance line. It is an algorithmic price magnet. The real edge does not come from the gap itself; it comes from knowing where price is going next on the higher timeframe and using the FVG as the low-risk transport vehicle to get there.
The Setup Rules
Identify the Higher-Timeframe Draw on Liquidity (DOL):
Before marking a single gap, determine where the market is reaching on the 4-hour or daily chart. Price must be moving toward an obvious buy-side liquidity pool (previous swing highs), sell-side liquidity pool (previous swing lows), or a higher-timeframe imbalance. Never take an FVG setup against the prevailing draw on liquidity.
Confirm a Liquidity Sweep and Market Structure Shift (MSS):
On your execution timeframe (typically the 15-minute, 5-minute, or 1-minute chart), wait for price to purge a key session high or low. Following that liquidity sweep, price must aggressively break through a previous swing point, establishing a confirmed Change of Character (CHOCH) or Market Structure Shift with a full candle body close.
Locate the Three-Candle Displacement Sequence:
Look at the energetic leg that produced the market structure shift. Candle 1 establishes the baseline wick extreme. Candle 2 must be an expansive displacement candle with a large body, tiny wicks, and significant relative volume. Candle 3 must close without its wick touching or overlapping Candle 1’s wick.
Measure the Imbalance Window and Consequent Encroachment:
Draw a horizontal zone from Candle 1’s wick extreme across to Candle 3’s wick extreme. In a bullish FVG, the lower boundary is Candle 1’s high and the upper boundary is Candle 3’s low. In a bearish FVG, the upper boundary is Candle 1’s low and the lower boundary is Candle 3’s high. Calculate the 50% midpoint of this shaded area, known as Consequent Encroachment (CE).
Ensure the Gap Is Fresh and Unmitigated:
The Fair Value Gap must remain unvisited by any subsequent candle wicks. If a subsequent candle has already traded into the gap and closed back outside it, that imbalance has already been mitigated and is no longer valid for a high-probability primary entry.
Entry Trigger
For an aggressive entry, place a limit order directly at the outer boundary of the Fair Value Gap (Candle 1’s high for bullish setups, or Candle 1’s low for bearish setups) as soon as the three-candle formation closes.
For a conservative entry, place a limit order precisely at the 50% Consequent Encroachment (CE) level of the gap, allowing for tighter risk parameters and a superior risk-to-reward ratio.
For a confirmation entry, wait for price to trade into the Fair Value Gap on your primary execution chart, drop down to the 1-minute chart, and pull the trigger only when an internal micro-market structure shift confirms algorithmic rejection from the zone.
Stop Loss & Profit Target
Risk management
on an ICT Fair Value Gap trade is mathematically fixed by the candles that create the imbalance, removing all emotional ambiguity.
Stop Loss Placement:
For a bullish setup, place your stop loss 2 to 3 pips below the absolute low of Candle 2 (the displacement candle). For a bearish setup, place your stop loss 2 to 3 pips above the absolute high of Candle 2. If price violates the origin of the displacement candle, the institutional thesis is immediately invalidated, and you must exit without hesitation.
Conservative Stop Alternative:
If Candle 2 is exceptionally large, you can place your stop loss 2 pips beyond the 50% midpoint of Candle 2’s body, or directly beyond the swing high or low that initiated the displacement move.
Profit Target Rules:
Target the next clean pool of liquidity on your chart. In a bullish trade, aim for equal highs, prior session highs, or an opposing bearish higher-timeframe FVG. In a bearish trade, aim for equal lows, prior session lows, or an opposing bullish FVG.
Risk/Reward Expectation:
Because entries occur inside the retracement phase of a displacement leg, your minimum risk-to-reward ratio should be 1:2.5, with well-selected setups frequently delivering 1:4 to 1:6 returns when capturing full expansion runs.

To see how this works in real market conditions, let us walk through a high-probability short trade on EUR/USD during the London-to-New York crossover.
As you can see in the chart below, EUR/USD was trending lower on the 4-hour chart, targeting an unliquidated pool of sell-side liquidity at 1.0790. During the London morning session, the pair rallied to retest the Asian session high at 1.0880, effectively sweeping buy-side liquidity and trapping breakout retail traders.
At 08:30 AM EST, following high-impact economic data, a massive wave of institutional selling hit the tape. On the 15-minute timeframe, Candle 1 printed between 1.0858 and 1.0850, ending with a low at 1.0850. Candle 2 then unleashed an 80-pip downward expansion, closing heavily near its low of 1.0815 and decisively shattering the prior 15-minute swing low at 1.0820, confirming an institutional Market Structure Shift. Candle 3 formed immediately after, pulling back slightly to a high of 1.0838 before continuing downward.

This price delivery left a clean, 12-pip unmitigated Bearish Fair Value Gap between the low of Candle 1 at 1.0850 and the high of Candle 3 at 1.0838. We marked this 12-pip zone and calculated the Consequent Encroachment (50% midpoint) at exactly 1.0844.
Rather than chasing the market down at 1.0815, we placed a sell limit order at the Consequent Encroachment level of 1.0844. We positioned our stop loss at 1.0862, which sat 2 pips above the high of Candle 1 and well protected above the origin of the displacement block. Our take profit order was set at 1.0790, aligning directly with the 4-hour sell-side liquidity pool.
Forty-five minutes later, price delivered a steady three-candle retracement directly into the Fair Value Gap. Price tapped 1.0844, filled our sell limit order perfectly, kissed 1.0847 without threatening our 1.0862 stop, and immediately printed an aggressive shooting star candle on the 5-minute chart. The balance was restored. Within the next two hours, the market dropped 54 pips straight into the 1.0790 target without a single structural pullback, closing out a pristine 1:3.2 risk-to-reward execution.
Common Mistakes to Avoid
Treating Every Three-Candle Gap as a Valid Setup:
An FVG that forms in the middle of a consolidation range without a prior liquidity sweep is noise. If price did not take out an old high or low before forming the gap, institutional sponsorship is absent.
Ignoring the Higher-Timeframe Trend and Bias:
Going long on a 5-minute bullish FVG when the 4-hour chart is aggressively cascading toward a daily sell-side liquidity target will destroy your account. The execution timeframe FVG must always align with the higher-timeframe draw on liquidity.
Demanding Exact Wick Touches on Massive Gaps:
If an FVG spans 30 to 40 pips on a volatile pair, waiting for price to touch the exact edge of Candle 1 often means missing the trade entirely. Use the 50% Consequent Encroachment level or scale your entries across the zone.
Holding Positions Through Opposing Imbalances:
Traders often turn winning trades into losers by refusing to take partial profits when price reaches an opposing higher-timeframe FVG. When price encounters an opposing imbalance, the algorithm often pauses or reverses. Take profits into the imbalance.
Trading Outside Institutional Killzones:
Fair Value Gaps that print during the late Asian session or the London lunch lull lack the institutional volume required for follow-through. Restrict your FVG execution exclusively to the London Open (02:00 – 05:00 EST) and New York Open (07:00 – 10:00 EST) Killzones.

Quick Reference Checklist
Is the higher-timeframe Draw on Liquidity (DOL) clearly identified on the 1-hour or 4-hour chart? (Yes/No)
Did the market sweep an opposing liquidity pool (previous day high/low, session high/low, or equal highs/lows) before reversing? (Yes/No)
Did the reversal produce an obvious Market Structure Shift (MSS) with a full candle body closing beyond a prior swing point? (Yes/No)
Is there a visible, non-overlapping gap between Candle 1’s wick and Candle 3’s wick surrounding an energetic displacement candle? (Yes/No)
Has the identified Fair Value Gap remained completely untouched and unmitigated by subsequent price action? (Yes/No)
Is your entry placed either at the FVG boundary or the 50% Consequent Encroachment line? (Yes/No)
Is your stop loss logically placed beyond Candle 2’s displacement high/low or behind the structural swing point? (Yes/No)
Does the trade offer a minimum calculated risk-to-reward ratio of at least 1:2.5 to your target liquidity pool? (Yes/No)
Are you trading strictly within an established ICT Killzone session window? (Yes/No)
Frequently Asked Questions
What is the difference between a Fair Value Gap and a Liquidity Void?
An ICT Fair Value Gap is a specific three-candle structural imbalance with defined boundaries between Candle 1 and Candle 3, whereas a liquidity void refers to a much larger, extended directional run characterized by multiple consecutive displacement candles that skip over significant trading volume across entire price ranges.
What does Consequent Encroachment mean in ICT trading?
Consequent Encroachment (CE) is the exact 50% mathematical midpoint of a Fair Value Gap or long-bodied displacement candle. Institutional algorithms frequently treat this midpoint as a primary equilibrium level where resting orders are cleared before price pivots sharply away.
Can an ICT Fair Value Gap fail, and what does it mean when it does?
Yes, an ICT Fair Value Gap fails when price blows completely through the zone and closes beyond Candle 1 with strong momentum. When an FVG is completely breached, it transitions into an Inversion Fair Value Gap (IFVG), reversing its role from support to resistance or vice versa for subsequent price tests.

Top 5 Best Pullback Trading Indicators for 2026: Catching the Snapback

All Trading Strategies
Pullback trading is a powerful strategy, letting traders enter strong trends at optimal points. Identifying those perfect dips before a snapback requires precise tools. This October 2026, we’re cutting through the noise to deliver the top 5 indicators on TradingView for nailing those entries in robust uptrends.
#5: Stochastic RSI
The
Stochastic
RSI
is a momentum oscillator derived from the Relative Strength Index (RSI). It smooths out the traditional RSI, making it more sensitive and quicker to react to market changes. Traders use it to identify overbought and oversold conditions within a trending market, looking for a bounce from oversold territory (typically below 20) as a pullback entry signal. It can be particularly effective in identifying short-term reversals in an established uptrend, giving a clear visual cue for potential dip buys. However, its high sensitivity means it generates many signals, leading to potential whipsaws if not used with a higher timeframe trend filter. Relying solely on its crossovers can be misleading without further confirmation, making it less ideal for beginners.
Pros:
Highly sensitive to price changes, effective for short-term overbought/oversold identification.
Cons:
Can generate frequent false signals and requires strong trend confirmation to be reliable.
#4: Williams %R
Williams %R is a momentum indicator that measures overbought and oversold levels, similar to the Stochastic Oscillator. It gauges the closing price relative to the high-low range over a specific period, typically 14 bars. Values between -20 and 0 are considered overbought, while values between -80 and -100 are oversold. For pullback strategies, traders watch for the indicator to dip into oversold territory (below -80) during an uptrend, signaling a potential entry point as the price consolidates before resuming its upward move. Its strength lies in its ability to quickly identify momentum shifts and potential reversals. However, like other oscillators, it can struggle in sideways markets and may provide early or false signals if not combined with trend analysis. It doesn’t offer native alert functionality on most platforms, requiring constant manual monitoring.
Pros:
Excellent for identifying short-term momentum shifts and potential oversold conditions.
Cons:
Can be prone to false signals in choppy markets and lacks built-in alerts for timely entry.
#3: Connors RSI (mean reversion oscillator)
The Connors RSI is a robust mean reversion oscillator that combines three components: a standard RSI, the number of consecutive up/down days, and the magnitude of price change over a short period. This multi-faceted approach aims to provide a more comprehensive view of momentum and overbought/oversold conditions, making it particularly useful for identifying exhaustion in pullbacks. For uptrend pullbacks, traders look for extreme low readings (e.g., below 10-20) as signals that the dip is overextended and a bounce is imminent. Its strength is its adaptive nature, capturing short-term sentiment effectively. However, it can be more complex to interpret than simpler oscillators, requiring a deeper understanding of its components and how they interact. Customization and backtesting are often needed to fine-tune its parameters for specific assets, which can be time-consuming for new traders.
Pros:
Combines multiple factors for a comprehensive view of momentum and mean reversion.
Cons:
More complex to interpret and optimize, potentially requiring extensive backtesting for effectiveness.
#2: Moving Average Pullback System (21 EMA bounce)
The
Moving Average
Pullback System, often centered around the 21 Exponential
Moving Average
(EMA), is a classic and highly effective strategy. In a strong uptrend, price frequently pulls back to touch or come close to a key moving average before bouncing higher. The 21 EMA is favored by many traders for its dynamic response to recent
price action

candlestick patterns
) as an entry signal. Its simplicity and visual clarity are significant advantages, making it easy to spot potential entry zones. A limitation is that the EMA itself is a lagging indicator; by the time price confirms a bounce, some of the initial move might be missed. It also doesn’t inherently distinguish between a pullback and a trend reversal, requiring additional confirmation from other trend filters or price action analysis.
Pros:
Simple, highly visual, and widely applicable for identifying dynamic support levels in trends.
Cons:
Lagging nature means missing early entry points and requires additional confirmation for trend continuity.

Why Standard Debit Spreads Fail in Choppy and High-Volatility Markets
Standard debit spreads require directional movement within a defined window of time to overcome the net debit paid. When price action turns erratic and non-directional, theta decay grinds down your long option faster than your short option can offset it.
High volatility compounds this issue by inflating option pricing across the chain, making directional missteps expensive. In choppy conditions, the underlying asset frequently whipsaws past your breakeven point without ever hitting your profit target.
Adjusting the trade is not about hoping for a miraculous trend reversal. Advanced adjustments alter the structure’s Greek profile, converting delta-heavy bets into delta-neutral, income-generating positions.
Adjustment 1: Converting a Stagnant Debit Spread into an Iron Condor
Converting a debit spread into an iron condor is the premier defense when your directional thesis stalls into a tight consolidation channel. This adjustment finances your original position by selling an out-of-the-money credit spread on the unthreatened side of the market.
If you hold an open Bull Call Spread that is drifting sideways, you sell an out-of-the-money Bear Put Spread below the current trading range. The credit harvested from the put spread immediately slashes the net debit of the original call spread.
This tactic creates a wide, profitable range between your short put and short call strikes. If the market continues to chop sideways through expiration, both the new credit spread and the long-short balance of your call spread work together to recover capital.
Worked Example: Transforming a Stalled Bull Call Spread
Let us look at a concrete adjustment scenario on SPY when price action grinds to a halt after entry. Say SPY trades at $500, and you anticipate an upward breakout.
You buy the 30-day $500 call for $9.50 and sell the $510 call for $4.50, paying a net debit of $5.00 ($500 per contract). Two weeks later, SPY sits stagnant at $502, implied volatility compresses, and your spread value drops to $3.60.
Instead of taking a $140 loss or waiting for expiration, you adjust the position into an iron condor by selling a put credit spread. You sell the 16-day SPY $492 put for $2.40 and buy the $482 put for $0.90, capturing a net credit of $1.50 ($150 per contract).

Adjustment 2: The Butterfly Morph (Squeezing the Short Strike)
The butterfly morph rescues a debit spread when the underlying asset moves swiftly toward your short strike, but implied volatility drops and leaves the trade stagnating before max profit. This is common during grind-up rallies where chop slows down upward velocity.
To execute this adjustment, you sell a second bear spread that shares your existing short strike. In a $500/$510 bull call spread, you sell an additional $510 call and buy a $520 call for a net credit.
Your position is now a $500/$510/$520 long call butterfly. You hold one long $500 call, two short $510 calls, and one long $520 call.
The credit collected from the second spread lowers your initial trade risk to near zero. You achieve peak profitability if the asset pins right at your central strike ($510) at expiration.
Adjustment 3: The Short Strike Scalp in Volatile Whipsaws
Whipsaw markets create sudden, aggressive price spikes that quickly retrace back into the trading channel. You can exploit these temporary volatility flashes by legging out of your short strike while leaving the long runner intact.
When the underlying asset dips violently against your bull call spread, your short call collapses in value, dropping to just 15% to 20% of its original premium. You buy back that short option cheaply, capturing nearly all of its theoretical profit.
You now hold a naked long call option paid for almost entirely by the short leg proceeds. When the market whipsaws back to the upside, you can either sell another out-of-the-money call at higher strikes or ride the long option back to breakeven.
This tactic requires strict technical triggers, such as an oversold bounce off a 50-day moving average or Bollinger Band extreme. Never attempt this maneuver unless you have predefined rules for where to re-hedge the long contract.
Common Mistakes to Avoid
The single most destructive adjustment mistake is adding fresh cash to a losing debit spread. Adjustments should remove capital from the table or reconfigure risk; they must never act as an excuse to double down on bad timing.
Another frequent trap is executing adjustments too early in the trade cycle. If you adjust the position on day three of a 45-day cycle because of minor noise, you destroy the trade’s structural edge before theta or delta can work.
Traders also commonly forget the assignment risk created by rolling short options too close to the current price. In volatile environments, high intrinsic value combined with upcoming dividend dates triggers early exercise, creating unwanted margin strain.

Can I adjust a debit spread into a credit spread?
Yes, you can morph a debit spread into a net credit position by rolling your short leg closer to the money or adding an opposing credit spread that pays more than your initial debit. This converts directional risk into a range-bound income trade.
When is it better to close a choppy debit spread instead of adjusting it?
Close the spread outright when the underlying asset cleanly breaks through your invalidation levels on heavy volume. Adjustments are meant to extract value from sideways consolidation, not to save trades where your core market thesis was fundamentally wrong.
Do advanced adjustments increase commission costs significantly?
Adjusting spreads requires opening additional contract legs, which generates extra broker commission and exchange fee expenses. Always ensure the credit received or risk reduced substantially outweighs the trading fees required to restructure the spread.

Complete Guide to Rising Three and Falling Three Methods
Ben Losier
October 22, 2026
Price Action Strategies
The rising three methods and falling three methods are five-candle continuation patterns signaling that an existing trend has paused for brief consolidation before resuming with institutional force. These patterns provide trend traders with structured, low-risk pullback entries directly within established momentum rather than forcing them to chase extended market swings. Whether you trade equities, forex, or futures, mastering these formations allows you to exploit shallow profit-taking phases while positioning ahead of the next directional expansion.
The rising three methods is a bullish continuation pattern, while the falling three methods is its bearish equivalent.
Both setups require five distinct candles: an initial trend-aligned impulse candle, three small counter-trend candles, and a final breakout candle.
The three middle candles must remain strictly contained within the high-and-low range of the initial anchor candle.
Volume typically contracts during the middle consolidation candles and spikes aggressively on the fifth breakout candle.
Entries trigger immediately upon the close of the fifth candle, with stops placed just beyond the consolidation extreme.
What Are the Rising and Falling Three Methods?
The rising three methods and falling three methods are five-candle continuation patterns first cataloged in classic Japanese candlestick analysis to mark orderly pullbacks within strong trends.
Developed by legendary 18th-century rice trader Munehisa Homma as part of Sakata’s Five Methods (Sakata Goho), these structures highlight a temporary interruption in market order flow. Rather than indicating an impending reversal, the pattern represents a controlled rest period where opposing traders attempt to push back but fail to make meaningful progress.

In technical analysis, continuation patterns are among the most reliable tools available because they align you directly with dominant momentum. Many inexperienced traders mistake the three internal counter-trend candles for the beginning of a market reversal. Professional operators, however, view this compact consolidation as passive absorption. When the fifth candle breaks out in the original direction, it confirms that counter-trend traders have been trapped and that the prevailing impulse is resuming.
The Market Psychology Behind Rising and Falling Three Methods
Market psychology within the rising and falling three methods reveals an aggressive institutional impulse followed by weak, low-conviction counter-trend speculation.
In a rising three methods setup, the first candle demonstrates unambiguous buyer control through a tall, decisive bullish body. Once that surge finishes, early buyers take partial profits while counter-trend scalpers attempt to short what they perceive to be an overextended market. This creates the three small, descending candles.
Notice what happens during those three pullback periods: the selling is lethargic. Sellers cannot even push the market below the low of the original green candle. Each of the three counter candles features a narrow range, indicating an absence of institutional selling volume. Institutional participants do not liquidate their core holdings; instead, they allow retail liquidity to accumulate within the first candle’s boundary.
By the fifth candle, counter-trend supply dries up completely. Institutional bids step back into the order book, creating a sudden imbalance that drives price aggressively upward. As the fifth candle surges past the high of the first candle, short sellers are forced to buy back their positions to cover losses, while breakout traders pile in simultaneously. This dual surge of buying pressure powers the next extended leg of the trend.
How to identify the pattern on a chart
How to Identify the Rising and Falling Three Methods on a Chart
Identifying the rising and falling three methods requires checking five consecutive candles against strict sizing, color, and range rules.
Because true five-candle continuation formations are rare compared to standard two-candle pullbacks, applying precise visual filters separates high-probability structures from noisy market chop.
The Anchor Candle (Candle 1)
Candle 1 must be a wide-range, trend-aligned impulse candle. In the rising three methods, Candle 1 is a tall bullish green bar reflecting dominant buying pressure. In the falling three methods, Candle 1 is a tall bearish red bar reflecting severe liquidation. The real body of this candle should be noticeably larger than the average candle size over the preceding ten periods, featuring minimal upper and lower wicks.
The Counter-Trend Consolidation (Candles 2, 3, and 4)
Candles 2, 3, and 4 form the internal correction. In the rising formation, these three candles generally have small bearish bodies that drift gradually downward. In the falling formation, they have small bullish bodies that drift gradually upward. Most importantly, the collective highs and lows of all three middle candles must remain entirely within the high-low range of Candle 1. If any middle candle closes beyond the extreme of Candle 1, the pattern is invalid.
The Resolution Candle (Candle 5)
Candle 5 provides the ultimate pattern confirmation. For the rising three methods, Candle 5 must open above the low of the preceding candle and close decisively above the close—and ideally above the absolute high—of Candle 1. For the falling three methods, Candle 5 must open below the high of the prior candle and close cleanly below the close and low of Candle 1. Candle 5 must demonstrate strong expansion, matching or exceeding Candle 1 in physical body size.
Location and Trend Context
Neither pattern can exist in isolation or within a horizontal trading range. The rising three methods must occur within an established sequence of higher highs and higher lows, ideally above a rising 20-period
moving average
. The falling three methods must appear within an established downtrend below a falling moving average. Context determines edge: a five-candle cluster matching this shape inside an indecisive range is merely consolidation, not a continuation pattern.
The Exact Rising and Falling Three Methods Setup Criteria
Every valid rising or falling three methods setup must satisfy five mandatory technical criteria before you risk capital.
If even one condition fails, step aside and wait for cleaner market structure.
Established Trend:
The market must trade in a confirmed trend, demonstrated by price holding consistently on the trending side of the 20-period Exponential Moving Average (EMA).
Anchor Body Dominance:
Candle 1 must possess a real body that is at least 1.5 times the 14-period
Average True Range
(ATR), showing clear institutional sponsorship.
Range Containment:
The high, low, open, and close of Candles 2, 3, and 4 must remain strictly between the high and low extremes of Candle 1.
Volume Contraction:
Trading volume must visibly diminish across Candles 2, 3, and 4, confirming that the counter-trend movement lacks institutional participation.
Decisive Breakout Close:
Candle 5 must close beyond the extreme of Candle 1 on expanding volume that equals or exceeds the volume recorded on Candle 1.
Trade setup: entry, stop loss, and profit target
How Do You Trade the Rising and Falling Three Methods? (Entry, Stop Loss, Target)
You trade the rising and falling three methods by entering on the close of the fifth candle, placing your protective stop beyond the consolidation extreme, and targeting a minimum 2:1 risk-to-reward ratio.
Executing this strategy demands discipline because entering on Candle 5 requires buying after a large up candle or selling short after a large down candle.
For the rising three methods, your standard entry executes at the market close of Candle 5 once it locks in a close above Candle 1’s high. Conservative traders can place a buy-stop order 1 tick above the high of Candle 5 to demand immediate follow-through. Anchor your stop loss 1 tick below the lowest point of the three-candle consolidation cluster (Candles 2 through 4). If that pullback was particularly shallow, place the stop loss 1 tick below the low of Candle 1 to prevent being shaken out by normal market noise.
For the falling three methods, enter short at the close of Candle 5 as soon as it prints a close below the low of Candle 1. Your stop loss rests 1 tick above the highest wick of Candles 2, 3, and 4. This placement ensures that if price breaks back above the counter-trend consolidation, your trade thesis is invalidated immediately with minimal capital loss.
To establish profit targets, employ a measured move technique. Calculate the vertical distance of the initial trend leg leading up to and including Candle 1, then project that identical distance outward from the low of Candle 4. Alternatively, target key multi-timeframe
support and resistance
levels that sit at least twice your initial risk distance away. Never accept an execution where the nearest structural barrier restricts your potential return to less than a 2:1 risk-to-reward ratio.

To understand the execution mechanics in a live trading environment, walk through this detailed hypothetical trade on a 4-hour chart.
Say an equity is trading in a clean uptrend, climbing steadily from $92.00 to $104.00 over several weeks while respecting the rising 20-period EMA.
As you can see in the annotated chart above, the pattern begins when buyers initiate a massive expansion candle (Candle 1) that opens at $104.20 and closes near its peak at $108.50. This candle prints an exceptionally tall body accompanied by high institutional trading volume. Over the next three 4-hour sessions,
price action
stalls. Candle 2 opens at $108.20 and closes down at $107.10. Candle 3 drifts lower, opening at $107.00 and closing at $106.20. Candle 4 prints another small red bar, touching an intraday low of $105.40 before settling at $105.80.
During these three sessions, two vital details emerge: first, the lowest price ($105.40) stays comfortably above the low of Candle 1 ($104.20); second, volume drops significantly with each successive session. Sellers are clearly unable to generate momentum.
Then Candle 5 forms. It opens at $106.00 and immediately attracts heavy buying volume. Over the 4-hour period, buyers push price aggressively through the highs of Candles 2, 3, and 4, ultimately closing at $109.10—surpassing the high of Candle 1 ($108.50). You execute a long entry at the candle close of $109.10. You anchor your stop loss at $105.30 (10 cents below the consolidation low of $105.40), risking $3.80 per share.
Using a measured move based on the initial impulse swing of $6.00 ($108.50 – $102.50), you project a profit target $7.60 above entry at $116.70, securing a precise 2:1 risk-to-reward ratio. Over the subsequent sessions, buyers drive price directly into your $116.70 target without the stop ever being threatened.

Rising and Falling Three Methods Across Different Timeframes
The rising and falling three methods appear on all chart intervals, but signal reliability increases substantially as you move toward higher timeframes.
Understanding how timeframe dynamics affect pattern fidelity helps you avoid false signals on volatile lower charts.
5-Minute Charts:
Intraday noise frequently distorts the five-candle structure. While patterns appear often, high-frequency algorithms and spread fluctuations often cause the middle candles to breach Candle 1’s extremes falsely. Only trade intraday setups that align directly with higher-timeframe key levels and broad market volume surges.
1-Hour Charts:
The 1-hour timeframe provides an exceptional balance between setup frequency and technical reliability. On this timeframe, the three consolidation candles often reflect a clean session transition, such as the quiet European midday lull following an active London open.
Daily Charts:
Daily charts yield the highest win rates for both rising and falling three methods. A three-day consolidation contained entirely within a prior expansion day represents genuine institutional position-building. False breakouts on daily charts are significantly less common.
Weekly Charts:
On weekly timeframes, the pattern maps major macroeconomic trend continuations. While setups occur rarely, their breakout phases typically trigger multi-month directional trends suitable for swing and position traders.
Rising Three Methods vs. Bull Flag: Key Differences
While both the rising three methods and the
bull flag
represent bullish continuation patterns, they differ fundamentally in candle constraints, duration, and visual geometry.
Many traders mistakenly use these terms interchangeably, leading to improper trade management.
Rising Three Methods
The rising three methods is a strict, five-candle
candlestick pattern
governed by rigid rules. It requires exactly one large bullish candle, precisely three smaller downward or horizontal candles that remain completely contained within the first candle’s extremes, and one final confirming breakout candle. The pattern operates across a fixed, narrow window of time (exactly five bars), making its invalidation criteria unambiguous.
Bull Flag
The bull flag is a broader chart pattern that consists of an impulsive advance (the flagpole) followed by a downward-sloping channel (the flag). Unlike the rising three methods, a bull flag has no fixed candle count; the consolidation phase can span anywhere from 5 to 25 candles or more. Furthermore, the retracement in a bull flag frequently retraces 38.2% to 50% of the flagpole, often extending well beyond the range of any single individual candle.
Best Confluences to Stack With Rising and Falling Three Methods
Stacking structural technical confluences with the rising and falling three methods dramatically increases trade execution quality.
Isolated
candlestick patterns
can fail; patterns that resolve at confluence zones rarely do.
20-Period Exponential Moving Average (EMA):
When the three counter-trend consolidation candles drift back and gently test a rising (or falling) 20 EMA before Candle 5 launches away, the moving average acts as dynamic support or resistance, confirming trend strength.
Prior Support Turned Resistance (and Vice Versa):
Look for Candle 1 to break through a key horizontal market level, followed by Candles 2 through 4 retesting that broken level from above or below. Candle 5 then confirms the successful retest.
Volume Profile Anchored
VWAP
:
Anchoring a Volume Weighted Average Price (VWAP) to the beginning of the impulse move provides an institutional benchmark. If the three consolidation candles hold above the anchored VWAP, buyers remain in control.
Higher-Timeframe Trend Alignment:
Taking a 1-hour rising three methods setup only when the daily chart is in a confirmed bullish market structure filters out deceptive counter-trend traps.
Common Rising and Falling Three Methods Mistakes to Avoid
Traders most often lose money on these patterns by relaxing visual rules and entering before the fifth candle confirms the setup.
Eliminate these specific errors from your trading routine:
Anticipating Candle 5:
Entering during Candles 2, 3, or 4 to get a better price is a common amateur mistake. Until Candle 5 closes outside the range of Candle 1, the pattern does not exist, and the pullback may easily develop into a full-scale trend reversal.
Tolerating Range Violations:
Allowing the wicks of the three consolidation candles to drift far above (in falling methods) or below (in rising methods) the boundaries of Candle 1 invalidates the setup. Range containment is the core mechanical foundation of this pattern.
Ignoring Volume Signatures:
Trading the pattern when the middle candles show heavy, expanding volume is hazardous. High volume during the retracement indicates aggressive institutional counter-trend participation, not passive profit-taking.
Placing Stops Inside the Consolidation:
Setting an ultra-tight stop loss inside the range of Candles 2 through 4 invites stop runs. Always anchor your protective stop outside the structural high or low of the formation.
Trading in Low-Volatility Chop:
Attempting to trade these patterns when the market is trapped inside a wide sideways range produces constant losses because continuation setups require existing momentum to follow through.

Frequently Asked Questions About Rising and Falling Three Methods
Can the middle consolidation have two or four candles instead of three?
While classical Japanese theory specifies exactly three counter-trend candles, real-world markets occasionally produce variations with two or four consolidation candles. As long as those candles remain strictly inside the high-low boundaries of Candle 1 on low volume, the underlying psychology remains valid. However, traditional five-candle formations offer the highest statistical reliability.
What is the best timeframe to trade rising and falling three methods?
The 1-hour and daily timeframes produce the cleanest and most dependable rising and falling three methods setups. Higher timeframes filter out intraday spread fluctuations and algorithmic noise, allowing true institutional accumulation and distribution to develop clearly on the chart.
Where exactly should my stop loss go when trading these patterns?
Your protective stop loss should sit 1 tick outside the lowest point of the three consolidation candles for a rising three methods setup, or 1 tick outside the highest point for a falling three methods setup. If the retracement is exceptionally shallow, placing the stop just beyond the outer boundary of Candle 1 provides additional structural protection.
What invalidates a rising or falling three methods pattern?
A pattern is invalidated if any of the middle consolidation candles closes beyond the extreme high or low of Candle 1, or if Candle 5 fails to close past Candle 1’s body. Additionally, if the three middle candles print on expanding volume higher than Candle 1, the pattern is compromised and should not be traded.

Kicker Candlestick Pattern: Ultimate Guide to Reversals
Ben Losier
October 20, 2026
Price Action Strategies
The kicker candlestick pattern is a two-bar reversal formation that signals an immediate, violent change in market direction. It forms when a trend-continuation candle is instantly followed by a candle opening at or beyond the prior candle’s open and driving aggressively in the opposite direction, leaving an open gap between the two real bodies. This guide details the exact structural rules, psychological drivers, and trade execution mechanics required to trade both bullish and bearish kicker patterns profitably.
The kicker candlestick pattern is widely considered the most aggressive and reliable two-candle reversal formation in technical analysis.
True kickers require two opposite-colored marubozu or near-marubozu candles separated by a price gap between their bodies.
The pattern represents an instantaneous institutional repricing event, typically triggered by unexpected fundamental catalysts or major liquidity shifts.
Valid kicker patterns do not allow price to fill the gap during the formation of the second candle.
Risk is clearly defined below or above the extreme of the second candle, offering asymmetrical reward-to-risk ratios.
What Is the Kicker Candlestick Pattern?
The kicker candlestick pattern is a rare, high-momentum two-candle reversal pattern characterized by a sudden price gap against the prevailing trend with zero body overlap.
First documented comprehensively by Steve Nison in his foundational work on Japanese charting techniques, the kicker pattern stands apart from virtually all other candlestick structures because it does not require a period of consolidation or indecision. Instead, the market snaps from extreme bullishness to extreme bearishness—or vice versa—in the span of a single candle transition.

In a downtrend, a bullish kicker starts with a heavy selling candle, only for the subsequent session to open at or above the prior candle’s open and surge upward to form a massive green candle. In an uptrend, a bearish kicker begins with a confident green candle, followed immediately by a session that gaps down to or below the prior open and collapses into a wide red candle. Traders monitor this setup because its completion demonstrates complete institutional surrender by one side and uncontested dominance by the other.
The Market Psychology Behind the Kicker Pattern
The psychology behind the kicker pattern centers on an immediate trap and liquidation cycle that forces one side of the market into instant capitulation.
In typical trend reversals, such as
double bottoms
or head-and-shoulders formations, buyers and sellers engage in an extended tug-of-war where momentum slowly decelerates. The kicker pattern features no deceleration; it represents total, instantaneous repricing.
Consider a stock trending lower where short sellers are comfortable and trend-following algorithms are actively pressing down. The first candle of the pattern prints a large red body, confirming to retail participants that the downtrend remains intact. Overnight or across the session break, material new information reaches the market—such as an earnings blowout, regulatory approval, or an institutional block buy. When trading resumes, the asset opens not just slightly higher, but completely above the prior session’s opening price.
Every single trader who sold short during the previous session is immediately underwater at the opening bell. Because the second candle opens and immediately drives higher without testing lower prices, trapped shorts cannot exit at breakeven. Their frantic buy-to-cover stop orders hit the market simultaneously with aggressive momentum buyers entering long. This dual buying pressure creates a self-fulfilling cascade of volume that leaves no room for price retracement.
How to identify the pattern on a chart
How to Identify the Kicker Candlestick Pattern on a Chart
To identify a valid kicker pattern, look for two large, opposing-color candles separated by a distinct price gap between their open prices, with virtually no shadows.
Because the kicker represents raw institutional order flow, sloppy candle structures that show long wicks or partial body overlap invalidate the setup entirely.
Candle One: Trend Continuation
The first candle must move cleanly in the direction of the established trend. If the market is moving downward, Candle One must be a long red candle with a wide real body. If the market is in an uptrend, Candle One must be an assertive, expansive green candle. The body must reflect unambiguous control by the prevailing trend participants.
The Separation Gap
The defining structural feature of the kicker pattern is the gap between the open of Candle One and the open of Candle Two. In a bullish kicker, Candle Two must open at or above the opening price of Candle One. In a bearish kicker, Candle Two must open at or below the opening price of Candle One. There must be zero overlap between the opening levels of the two candles.
Candle Two: The Counter-Strike
Candle Two must print an aggressive real body in the opposite direction of Candle One. For a bullish kicker, Candle Two is a large green candle that closes near its absolute high. For a bearish kicker, Candle Two is a large red candle that closes near its absolute low. The range of Candle Two should equal or exceed the range of Candle One.
Shadow Length and Wicks
Both candles in a textbook kicker pattern should resemble marubozu candles, meaning they feature tiny or nonexistent upper and lower wicks. A long wick on Candle Two indicates that the opposing side fought back, which dilutes the single-direction violence that makes the kicker reliable. A lower wick on a bullish Candle Two that dips into the prior session’s body range instantly negates the pattern.
The Exact Kicker Pattern Setup Criteria
A valid kicker setup requires strict adherence to structural geometry, timeframe selection, and volume expansion.
Every single condition below must be verified before executing a position:
Preceding Trend Context:
The pattern must appear within a clearly defined trend or after a persistent multi-bar swing. A kicker appearing in the middle of a choppy, low-volatility trading range is simply erratic
price action
and should be skipped.
Candle One Integrity:
Candle One must be a solid marubozu or near-marubozu in the direction of the trend, closing within the top or bottom 10% of its total range.
Gap Placement:
Candle Two must gap open at or beyond the opening price of Candle One. A gap that only clears the previous close does not satisfy the criteria of a true kicker.
Zero Retracement:
Price must not retrace back into the gap during the formation of Candle Two. The open of Candle Two must remain its absolute low (in a bullish kicker) or its absolute high (in a bearish kicker), with an allowable wick tolerance of no more than 5% of the total candle body.
Volume Confirmation:
Volume on Candle Two must be noticeably higher than the 20-period volume average, ideally outpacing Candle One by at least 1.5x. Heavy volume confirms institutional sponsorship.
Timeframe Suitability:
The pattern must be traded on higher timeframes, primarily the daily or 4-hour charts, where gaps reflect real market-wide shifts rather than microstructural illiquidity.
Trade setup: entry, stop loss, and profit target
How Do You Trade the Kicker Candlestick Pattern? (Entry, Stop Loss, Target)
Trade the kicker pattern by entering on the close of Candle Two, anchoring your stop loss just beyond Candle Two’s extreme, and targeting uncollected liquidity pools for a minimum 2:1 reward-to-risk ratio.
Because kickers move aggressively, waiting for a retest of the gap often results in missed trades, as high-quality kickers rarely look back.
The Entry Trigger
When I trade this setup, I enter via a market order directly on the close of Candle Two once its structural validity is locked. Aggressive traders sometimes enter right as Candle Two crosses the high of Candle One early in the session, but waiting for the candle close eliminates the risk of an intraday fade that leaves an invalid wick.
Stop Loss Placement
Your protective stop loss must be placed 1
average true range
(ATR) or a few ticks beyond the opening price (or wick extreme) of Candle Two. In a bullish kicker, the stop loss sits just below the low of Candle Two. If price breaches the open of Candle Two, the entire psychological premise of institutional urgency is broken, and you must exit immediately without hesitation.
Take Profit Targets and Risk-to-Reward
Kickers routinely launch extended multi-day or multi-week trends. Set your first take-profit target at the nearest major structural swing high or swing low, aiming for an initial reward-to-risk (R:R) ratio of at least 2:1. When price reaches a 1.5R gain, move your stop loss to breakeven and trail the remaining position behind successive 20-period exponential
moving average
(EMA) closes or key swing fractals.

Kicker Pattern Trade Example: Step-by-Step
Let us walk through a concrete, step-by-step hypothetical trade on a stock trading at $50.00 following an extended multi-week decline.
As you can see in the annotated chart above, the stock has been grinding downward from $65.00, carving out consistent lower highs and lower lows along its downward-sloping 20-period EMA.
On Day 1, the sellers push the stock down into a fresh swing low. The candle opens at $52.00 and sells off aggressively throughout the session, closing right at the dead low of $50.00 with virtually zero lower wick. Total volume prints at 1.2 million shares, matching the daily average. Bears are firmly in control, and the technical picture suggests another leg down toward the $45.00 support level.
On Day 2, before the opening bell, the company announces a major enterprise contract that alters its revenue outlook. The stock opens at $52.50—a massive gap that opens above Day 1’s open of $52.00. At the bell, buyers flood the order book. Price never trades below $52.40, printing a tiny 10-cent lower shadow before ripping directly upward through the morning session.
By the closing bell of Day 2, the stock finishes at $56.00, printing a massive green marubozu. The volume registers 3.8 million shares—over three times the 20-day average. The kicker pattern criteria are fully met: Candle One was a full red body, Candle Two gapped above Candle One’s open, there was zero retest of the gap, and volume exploded.
I enter long at the market close of Day 2 at $56.00. My stop loss is placed 25 cents below the low of Candle Two at $52.15, giving a total trade risk of $3.85 per share. The Kicker Pattern Across Different Timeframes
The daily chart produces the cleanest and most reliable kicker signals because it reflects true inter-session repricing backed by fundamental catalysts.
Applying the pattern across different chart intervals alters its reliability and technical meaning dramatically.
The 5-Minute Chart
On low timeframes like the 5-minute chart, genuine gaps between candle bodies rarely occur during regular trading hours unless high-impact economic data prints (such as CPI or central bank decisions). Most apparent gaps on 5-minute charts are simply manifestations of thin order books or spread widening. Trading kickers on 5-minute charts yields high slippage and frequent false breakouts. Reserve this pattern for higher time horizons.
The 1-Hour and 4-Hour Charts
On the 1-hour and 4-hour charts, kickers frequently appear around session handoffs—such as the transition from the Asian to London session, or London to New York. When an institutional block order hits during a session open, it can create a genuine intraday kicker. These setups carry solid momentum and offer high-probability day trades or short-term swing positions.
The Daily Chart
The daily timeframe is the gold standard for the kicker candlestick pattern. Overnight corporate earnings, geopolitical developments, and macroeconomic shifts generate true, unfillable opening gaps that trap entire market participant classes. Daily kickers routinely mark durable cycle bottoms and cycle tops that persist for weeks.
The Weekly Chart
Weekly kickers are exceptionally rare, appearing only a handful of times per decade across broad market indices or large-cap equities. When a weekly kicker does print, it signals a generational macroeconomic regime change. The trade duration for weekly kickers spans months to quarters, requiring wide stop losses and adjusted
position sizing
.
Bullish Kicker vs. Bullish Engulfing: Key Differences
The primary difference between a bullish kicker and a
bullish engulfing
pattern is the location of Candle Two’s opening price relative to Candle One.
Traders frequently confuse these two powerful reversal patterns, but their underlying structural mechanics are fundamentally distinct.
Bullish Kicker Pattern
In a bullish kicker, Candle Two opens with a gap up, starting at or above the opening price of the previous red candle. The real bodies of the two candles never touch or occupy the same vertical price zone. This gap signifies an overwhelming, instantaneous imbalance of buyers over sellers before trading even opens. It is a pure momentum surge requiring zero period of price discovery.
Bullish Engulfing Pattern
In a bullish engulfing pattern, Candle Two opens below the closing price of Candle One, temporarily suggesting that the downtrend is continuing. Only after the open do buyers step in, driving price up to swallow or engulf the entire body of Candle One. The bullish engulfing pattern is an intraday turnaround, whereas the kicker is an inter-session dislocation. While both are powerful, the kicker pattern reflects substantially more violent institutional urgency.
Best Confluences to Stack With the Kicker Pattern
Stacking structural technical confluences with a kicker pattern eliminates low-probability noise and filters for trades with institutional backing.
Look for these specific conditions to validate your trade:
Key Horizontal Support or Resistance Levels:
When a bullish kicker forms directly on a multi-month horizontal support level, it confirms that institutions defended that zone with aggressive limit orders and immediate market buy programs.
The 200-Period Exponential Moving Average (EMA):
A kicker pattern that gaps back across the 200-period EMA signals that a long-term macro trend is reclaiming its structural bias, triggering algorithmic momentum funds to enter.
Volume Spikes Above the 20-Day Average:
Candle Two must print volume that dwarfs recent trading activity; a low-volume kicker is an immediate red flag that suggests lack of institutional participation.
Prior Day High/Low Violations:
A kicker that not only gaps above the open of Candle One but also clears the high of the past three to five sessions simultaneously triggers cascading breakout stops that accelerate the trade.
Volume Weighted Average Price (
VWAP
) Dislocation:
If an intraday kicker gaps completely above the upper multi-day VWAP standard deviation band, it signals an extreme expansion phase that rarely reverts to the mean quickly.
Common Kicker Pattern Mistakes to Avoid
The most catastrophic mistake traders make with the kicker pattern is attempting to fade the gap under the assumption that all price gaps must fill.
Avoid these specific execution traps:
Fading the Pattern for a Gap Fill:
Retail traders often attempt to counter-trend trade a kicker, expecting price to drop and close the gap. Kicker gaps are breakaway gaps; fading them often results in devastating losses as the market trends relentlessly against the trapped position.
Trading Low-Liquidity Equities:
In micro-cap stocks or illiquid crypto pairs, gaps occur naturally due to wide bid-ask spreads rather than institutional order flow. A kicker is only statistically meaningful in liquid instruments where tight spreads are standard.
Entering Before Candle Two Closes:
Buying mid-session because price looks like a kicker exposes you to massive intraday wick rejections. If buyers fail to hold the advance and the candle closes with a long upper wick, the kicker is completely invalidated.
Placing Stops Inside the Gap:
Positioning your protective stop inside the open space between the two candle bodies gives the market a high-probability target to hunt during initial volatility. Anchor your stop strictly behind the low (or high) of Candle Two.
Ignoring Market Regimes:
Trading a single-stock bullish kicker when the broad market index is gapping down into an aggressive liquidation day lowers the probability of follow-through. Always align the setup with broad sector and index direction.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Kicker Pattern
How reliable is the kicker candlestick pattern?
The kicker candlestick pattern is widely considered one of the highest-probability two-candle reversal patterns in technical analysis.
Its high reliability stems from the fact that it requires a massive, unfillable gap against an established trend, which only occurs when institutional capital aggressively reallocates. When backed by above-average volume on daily charts, false signals are rare compared to standard
candlestick patterns
.
What is the difference between a kicker and an island reversal?
An island reversal consists of several consolidation candles isolated by two separate gaps, whereas a kicker pattern consists of just two back-to-back candles.
In an island reversal, price gaps in the trend direction, trades within a tight range for several days, and then gaps back in the opposite direction. A kicker reverses immediately on the very next session with a single gap, reflecting far more urgent sentiment change.
Can a kicker candlestick pattern occur in intraday trading?
Yes, kicker patterns can form on intraday charts, but they are only reliable during major session opens or after high-impact news releases.
Most intraday gaps on lower timeframes like the 1-minute or 5-minute charts are artifacts of bid-ask spread widening rather than genuine institutional revaluations. For best results, stick to the 1-hour, 4-hour, and daily timeframes.
Where should I place my stop loss when trading a kicker pattern?
Place your stop loss approximately 1 ATR or a few ticks beyond the opening price or wick extreme of Candle Two.
If price retreats enough to cross the open of Candle Two and penetrate the gap, the violent institutional momentum that defines the kicker pattern has failed. Exiting immediately preserves your trading capital for valid setups.

What Is a Fair Value Gap (FVG) in Trading? Complete ICT Guide
Ben Losier
September 15, 2026
Trading Strategies
A
Fair Value Gap
(FVG) is a three-candle price pattern representing an imbalance caused by aggressive institutional buying or selling. It appears on a chart as an open pocket of price action between the wick of the first candle and the wick of the third candle, leaving the middle candle largely one-sided. Traders use FVGs because price frequently acts like a magnet toward these inefficiencies, pulling back to rebalance orders before resuming the dominant trend.
Three-Candle Structure:
An FVG always requires a sequence of three consecutive candles, isolating an imbalance between the first and third wicks.
Institutional Footprint:
The pattern highlights rapid displacement where algorithms push price through levels without allowing two-sided trade execution.
Magnet Effect:
Unfilled FVGs act as high-probability targets for retracements because the market seeks equilibrium.
Consequent Encroachment:
The exact 50% midpoint of the gap serves as a critical institutional reaction level.
Directional Bias:
An FVG is only reliable when it forms in the direction of the higher-timeframe order flow.
What Is a Fair Value Gap?
A Fair Value Gap occurs when heavy volume floods the market, causing a one-directional price expansion that skips resting limit orders. In Inner Circle Trader (ICT) methodology, this phenomenon reflects an inefficient delivery of price where smart money participants distribute contracts without letting opposite orders match. When liquidity is skipped, the algorithm leaves behind a vacuum. Price tends to return to these vacuums to auction fairly across the neglected price levels before continuing in the direction of original displacement.
What a Fair Value Gap Looks Like on a Chart
An FVG is strictly visual and requires three candles to form. Here is the exact identification criteria:
Candle 1 (The Anchor):
This candle establishes the baseline. In a bullish FVG, its upper wick marks the lower boundary of the gap. In a bearish FVG, its lower wick marks the upper boundary.
Candle 2 (The Displacement):
A large, aggressive expansion candle with a wide body and short wicks. This candle must move decisively away from Candle 1.
Candle 3 (The Confirmation):
The candle immediately following the expansion. In a bullish FVG, the low of Candle 3 must not overlap the high of Candle 1. In a bearish FVG, the high of Candle 3 must not overlap the low of Candle 1.
The Imbalance Zone:
The empty space between Candle 1’s wick and Candle 3’s wick is the actual Fair Value Gap. As long as no wicks or candle bodies have filled that space, the FVG remains open and active.
How Traders Use Fair Value Gaps
FVGs provide objective, rules-based entry zones when paired with higher-timeframe directional bias. Rather than chasing strong moves, disciplined traders wait for the rebalance.
Establish Market Context:
Identify the broader trend and locate the next draw on liquidity, such as an old swing high, swing low, or opposing pool of stops.
Identify the Displacement:
Spot an energetic break in market structure that leaves behind a clean three-candle FVG in the direction of your bias.
Mark the Rebalance Levels:
Plot the opening edge of the gap and its Consequent Encroachment (the 50% level of the gap’s range).
Wait for Retracement:
Allow price to retrace into the FVG. Aggressive entries trigger at the front edge of the gap; conservative entries trigger at the 50% level.
Define Invalidation and Targets:
Place a stop loss beyond the low of Candle 2 (for a bullish setup) or above the high of Candle 2 (for a bearish setup). Target opposing liquidity pools or subsequent swing points.

Fair Value Gap vs. Order Block
Traders often confuse Fair Value Gaps with Order Blocks, yet they represent two distinct market mechanics. An
Order Block
is the final opposing candle before an aggressive expansion—it represents institutional order accumulation and mitigation. A
Fair Value Gap
is the literal void created during that expansion due to thin liquidity. While an Order Block highlights
who
initiated the move, the FVG marks
where
price was delivered inefficiently. High-probability setups frequently occur when an Order Block and an FVG sit directly adjacent to each other, creating an overlapping zone of high institutional interest.
Frequently Asked Questions
Does every Fair Value Gap get filled?
No. Price does not guarantee an immediate or total fill of every FVG. Runaway trends frequently leave gaps completely untouched, which confirms extraordinary institutional momentum in that direction.
What is Consequent Encroachment (CE)?
Consequent Encroachment is the exact 50% midpoint of a Fair Value Gap. Smart money algorithms often use this level as an optimal rebalance point, treating it as support or resistance before reversing.
Which timeframe works best for Fair Value Gaps?
FVGs are fractal, meaning they appear and work on every timeframe from the monthly down to the 1-minute chart. Higher-timeframe FVGs carry significantly more weight and dictate the general direction, while lower-timeframe gaps refine entry timing.
What happens when an FVG gets completely violated?
When price blasts straight through an FVG without respecting it, the gap flips in polarity. This creates an Inversion Fair Value Gap (IFVG), where an old bullish imbalance transforms into future bearish resistance, or vice versa.

The ICT Inversion
Fair Value Gap
(IFVG) in Crude Oil (CL) futures exploits institutional order flow when a standard three-candle imbalance fails to hold and flips polarity. Built for active futures day traders, this strategy converts failed support into dominant resistance—or failed resistance into explosive support—giving you razor-sharp entries during high-volatility intraday expansion legs.

What Is ICT Inversion Fair Value Gap?
An Inversion Fair Value Gap (IFVG) occurs when price completely invalidates an existing Fair Value Gap (FVG) and subsequently uses that exact price window as support or resistance in the opposite direction. Developed by Michael J. Huddleston (The Inner Circle Trader), the IFVG recognizes that an unfilled or violated imbalance reveals aggressive institutional repricing. Instead of looking for price to respect the original three-candle inefficiency, you wait for smart money to blow through it and trade the retest of the inverted zone.
Why This Edge Works
Crude oil is one of the most momentum-driven, liquidity-sensitive commodities in the world. When commercial hedgers and algorithmic desks build positions, they regularly engineer liquidity sweeps that trap retail breakout traders. A standard bearish FVG represents an area where sellers previously held an imbalance; when price punches straight through that zone with heavy volume and body-candle displacement, it reveals that buyers overpowered the resting sell orders.
Those trapped sellers now sit in underwater short positions. When price pulls back to the original imbalance, two mechanical events happen simultaneously: trapped shorts buy to break even, and momentum algorithms step in to defend the new institutional order flow. This structural shift flips the imbalance into an Inversion FVG, sparking aggressive continuation runs with minimal drawdown.
The Setup Rules
Market & Timeframe:
Trade exclusively on the Crude Oil (CL) futures contract using a 5-minute or 3-minute execution chart, aligned with the 15-minute or 1-hour trend bias.
Time of Day (Killzone):
Setup must form inside high-volume windows: London Open (2:00 AM – 5:00 AM EST) or the New York Energy Open / ICT NY Killzone (8:30 AM – 11:00 AM EST).
Identify the Initial Imbalance:
Spot a clean, standard three-candle Fair Value Gap (FVG) created against the higher-timeframe trend or during a recent liquidity run.
Wait for Displacement Inversion:
Look for a large, energetic candle to push completely through the FVG. The candle body must close entirely beyond the opposite boundary of the original gap, invalidating it.
Zone Confirmation:
Convert the invalidated FVG box into an Inversion FVG (IFVG). A failed bearish FVG now serves as a bullish support block; a failed bullish FVG becomes bearish overhead resistance.
Wait for the Retest:
Allow price to retrace back into the boundaries of the newly formed IFVG without letting a candle body close back outside the far boundary.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
Front-Running the Inversion:
Entering a trade while price is still inside the original FVG before a full candle body has closed beyond the zone. If it hasn’t closed through, it isn’t an inversion; it’s just a normal FVG that might reject you.

Ignoring Candle Body Closures:
Accepting setups where only long wicks breach the FVG. Wicks indicate rejection, not displacement. Valid inversions demand full candle body dominance through the imbalance.
Disregarding Higher Timeframe Context:
Buying a bullish IFVG directly into a higher timeframe 1-hour or daily bearish order block. Always ensure the local expansion path has clear room to run toward opposing liquidity.

is the final opposing candle or cluster of candles printed immediately before an aggressive, impulsive expansion in price that breaks market structure. It marks the structural footprint where institutional market participants heavily accumulated or distributed positions before forcing price away from equilibrium. Smart money traders identify these zones as primary supply and demand areas where price routinely returns to mitigate risk and trigger trend continuations.
Institutional footprint:
An order block represents the origin zone where large institutional volume entered the market prior to a decisive structural break.
Directional signature:
A bullish order block is the last down-close candle before an upward expansion, while a bearish order block is the last up-close candle before a downward plunge.
Mandatory displacement:
A valid order block requires an immediate, violent departure in price that creates a Fair Value Gap and a verified Market Structure Shift (MSS).
Mean threshold:
The 50% midpoint of the order block candle body acts as the critical defense level for institutional positions.
Clear invalidation:
If a candle body closes decisively past the extreme boundary of an order block, the setup is considered broken and invalidated.
What Is an ICT Order Block?
An Order Block is a specialized supply or demand zone representing concentrated institutional orders left behind before a violent directional shift. In modern ICT (Inner Circle Trader) theory, smart money entities do not enter positions with a single click; they must engineer liquidity and absorb opposing volume over several candles. When institutions execute these large orders, the immediate aftermath is an imbalance that rapidly displaces price away from that accumulation point.
This aggressive departure leaves the initial counter-orders placed by those institutions exposed. When price retraces back into that specific origin candle, institutions defend their cost basis, absorb any remaining retail liquidity, and fuel the next wave of trend continuation. Recognizing order blocks allows independent traders to align entries directly with institutional order flow rather than reacting to retail lag indicators.
What an Order Block Looks Like on a Chart
Identifying an authentic order block requires strict visual criteria rather than treating every turning point candle as a valid setup. Look for these clear visual steps:
The Origin Candle:
In a bullish market, identify the lowest bearish (down-close) candle formed before the start of an upward run. In a bearish market, identify the highest bullish (up-close) candle formed before a downward move.
The Displacement Leg:
Price must leave the origin candle with large, expanded range bodies. Small, overlapping candles disqualify the setup because they reflect indecision rather than institutional sponsorship.
Fair Value Gap Generation:
The displacement run must leave an open three-candle imbalance (Fair Value Gap) directly above a bullish block or below a bearish block.
Market Structure Shift (MSS):
The expansion run must cleanly break past a prior swing high or swing low with full candle bodies closing beyond the swing point.
The Protected Range:
The order block encompasses the full span from open to the extreme wick, with primary focus placed on the candle open and the 50% midpoint of the candle body (the mean threshold).
How Traders Use Order Blocks
Trading order blocks successfully requires patience and strict sequence verification.
Smart money concepts
demand execution only after the market confirms institutional intent.
Determine higher timeframe direction:
Establish the prevailing directional bias on the daily or 4-hour chart before looking for execution setups on lower timeframes.
Wait for structural displacement:
Allow price to sweep liquidity, produce a market structure shift, and print an obvious displacement move away from the turning point.
Map the order block boundaries:
Draw your zone from the open of the origin candle to its extreme wick, noting the 50% mean threshold as a high-reaction trigger level.
Execute on return to origin:
Enter your position when price retraces into the order block zone, targeting either the candle open or the mean threshold.
Set risk and profit targets:
Place your stop-loss just outside the extreme wick of the order block, and target resting buy-side or sell-side liquidity pools at opposing swing levels.

Order Block vs. Breaker Block
Traders often confuse order blocks with breaker blocks, but their functions in price delivery are polar opposites. An order block relies on
holding price structure
, while a breaker block forms after price
violates and flips
a prior order block.
An order block is expected to support price on its first retest and send the market forward to fresh highs or lows. In contrast, a breaker block occurs when a valid order block fails to hold, allows price to sweep an opposing liquidity extreme, and then gets aggressively shattered in reverse. Once price breaks through that failed order block, the zone flips polarity: a failed bullish order block becomes a bearish breaker block that repels price from underneath. In short, order blocks ride initial structural momentum, whereas breaker blocks capitalize on failed reversals and structural traps.

Which candle is the actual order block?
For a bullish setup, the order block is the final down-close (bearish) candle before the upward expansion leg begins. For a bearish setup, it is the final up-close (bullish) candle before the downward plunge. If two consecutive candles form the final leg of the swing before displacement, traders treat both bodies as a single consolidated order block.
What is the mean threshold of an order block?
The mean threshold is the exact 50% midpoint of the order block candle’s body or total range. Institutional traders view this level as the true balance point of the block. A candle body closing beyond the mean threshold serves as an early warning that the order block is losing momentum and may fail.
Can an order block be traded multiple times?
The highest-probability trade always occurs on the first return to the zone, known as the virgin mitigation. Each subsequent return to the same block weakens it because the institutional resting orders have already been absorbed. After one clean reaction, subsequent visits to the zone carry significantly higher failure rates.
How do I know if an order block is invalid?
An order block is invalid if price closes a full candle body beyond its outer wick extreme on the timeframe where the block was identified. Furthermore, if the move immediately following the block fails to produce an energetic displacement or fails to break a structural swing point, the candle lacks institutional backing and should be ignored.

. Additionally, retail supply and demand zones often treat wide zones as blanket entry areas, whereas ICT traders zero in on mathematically precise references: the
candle open
and the
mean threshold
.
Frequently Asked Questions
Which candle is the actual order block?
In a bullish setup, the order block is the lowest down-close candle directly preceding the upward displacement leg. In a bearish setup, it is the highest up-close candle before the aggressive downward expansion. If multiple consecutive candles print in that direction before the impulse, the entire sequence can be treated as a single order block zone.
What is the mean threshold of an order block?
The mean threshold is the exact 50% midpoint of the order block’s candle body, measured from open to close. Algorithmic order flow tends to defend this level strictly in high-probability trends. A candle body closing beyond the mean threshold usually signals that the block has failed.
Does an order block require a Fair Value Gap to be valid?
Yes, a genuine order block demands displacement that produces a
Fair Value Gap
. Without an imbalance, the counter-trend candle simply represents routine price consolidation rather than institutional manipulation. The presence of an imbalance proves that genuine institutional volume stepped into the market.
Can an order block be traded multiple times?
An order block carries the highest probability during its very first retest, also known as the initial mitigation. Once the block is mitigated and institutional orders are filled, its validity diminishes drastically. Subsequent retests carry a substantially higher probability of blowing through the level.

What Is ICT Silver Bullet?
The ICT Silver Bullet is a structured, session-specific intraday strategy created by Michael J. Huddleston, the founder of the Inner Circle Trader (ICT) methodology. The strategy isolates three precise 60-minute time intervals every trading day where algorithmic price delivery engines (IPDA) routinely seek liquidity and reprice imbalances.

Unlike discretionary breakout or indicator-based models, the Silver Bullet does not rely on
moving averages
, oscillators, or overnight swing projections. It operates entirely on liquidity runs, market structure shifts, and the rebalancing of a
Fair Value Gap
(FVG) formed inside designated hourly killzones. When executed on 1-minute to 5-minute charts, it provides intraday scalpers and day traders with a mechanical, repeatable framework.
Why This Edge Works
The edge of the ICT Silver Bullet strategy rests on institutional order delivery mechanics rather than retail chart patterns. Institutional order flow operates on predetermined liquidity schedules. Central bank dealers and institutional automated algorithms do not accumulate or distribute volume evenly across a 24-hour day. They execute during liquidity injections when interbank participation peaks.
During the Silver Bullet windows, interbank pricing algorithms systematically clear accumulated retail stop orders—known as Buy-Side Liquidity (BSL) and Sell-Side Liquidity (SSL). Once these retail stops are swept, the algorithm has the liquidity required to reverse price or violently continue the higher-timeframe trend.
When this repricing occurs with significant institutional displacement, it leaves behind an imbalance in the form of a Fair Value Gap. This three-candle formation represents inefficient price delivery where only one side of the market received orders. The algorithm systematically returns to rebalance that imbalance to neutral market efficiency before driving toward the next opposing pool of liquidity. By aligning your entry with this rebalancing phase inside the correct hour, you trade directly alongside interbank algorithmic execution.
The Setup Rules
Lock in the Exact Time Window:
Trade only during one of the three daily Silver Bullet intervals (all times in New York EST): the London Open window (3:00 AM – 4:00 AM), the New York AM window (10:00 AM – 11:00 AM), or the New York PM window (2:00 PM – 3:00 PM). Avoid entering before the hour begins or holding new entries past the 60-minute cutoff.
Identify the Higher-Timeframe Draw on Liquidity (DOL):
Before opening the 1-minute or 5-minute execution chart, inspect the 15-minute or 1-hour chart to determine where price wants to go. The draw must be an obvious opposing liquidity pool (previous daily high/low, session high/low, or equal highs/lows) or an unmitigated higher-timeframe Fair Value Gap.
Wait for a Liquidity Sweep:
Within the 60-minute window, observe price sweeping an internal or external liquidity level. For a bearish setup, price must sweep a prior swing high (Buy-Side Liquidity). For a bullish setup, price must sweep a prior swing low (Sell-Side Liquidity).
Confirm a Market Structure Shift (MSS) with Displacement:
After the liquidity sweep, look for an aggressive impulse move in the opposite direction. This move must decisively close beyond the most recent swing point on the 1-minute to 5-minute chart, producing large-bodied candles that prove institutional participation.
Isolate a Pristine Fair Value Gap (FVG):
The displacement leg must leave a distinct three-candle imbalance. In a bearish setup, candle 1’s low and candle 3’s high must not overlap, leaving an open zone in candle 2. In a bullish setup, candle 1’s high and candle 3’s low must remain separated.

Entry Trigger
An entry triggers the moment price retraces into the Fair Value Gap generated by the Market Structure Shift inside the active 60-minute Silver Bullet window.
You can execute this trigger in one of two ways depending on your execution discipline:
Limit Entry at the FVG Boundary:
Set a limit order at the outer boundary of the Fair Value Gap (the low of candle 1 in a bullish setup, or the high of candle 1 in a bearish setup). This ensures you enter early if price merely taps the gap.
Consequent Encroachment (CE) Limit Entry:
Place your limit order at the 50% midpoint of the Fair Value Gap box. This provides a tighter stop loss and superior risk-to-reward ratio, though you risk missing the trade if price only tags the edge before moving.
Never chase price if it runs away from the Fair Value Gap. If price moves into your profit target without retracing into the imbalance, the setup is dead; cancel the order and wait for the next session window.
Stop Loss & Profit Target
For stop loss placement, place your stop order exactly 1 to 2 pips beyond the swing high or swing low formed during the displacement move. Alternatively, if the displacement swing is unusually large, place your stop 1 pip beyond the invalidation level of the Fair Value Gap (the high of candle 1 for a short, or the low of candle 1 for a long). If price trades through that point, the thesis is structurally invalidated.
For profit targets, the ICT Silver Bullet is calibrated around a fixed baseline of 10 to 15 pips in major forex pairs. This aligns with standard institutional expansion runs during these windows. When trading pairs with higher average true ranges like GBP/USD, stretch your target to the next obvious internal liquidity pool (such as the opposing session swing low or an unmitigated opposite FVG) while locking in a minimum risk-to-reward ratio of 1:2.

Guide to SMC Change of Character (CHoCH) in Forex
Ben Losier
October 12, 2026
ICT vs SMC
A
Smart Money Concepts
(SMC) Change of Character (CHoCH) is the earliest structural signal that institutional order flow has shifted from trend continuation to trend reversal. It works because it tracks the exact moment smart money absorbs retail liquidity at swing extremes and commits heavy capital in the opposite direction. If you trade forex intraday or swing trade major currency pairs, mastering this single
price action
mechanism separates high-probability reversal entries from catastrophic fakeout traps.

What Is SMC Change of Character?
A Change of Character (CHoCH) is an SMC market structure pattern where price violates the most recent counter-trend swing high or swing low following a significant liquidity sweep. Popularized through institutional price action frameworks and Smart Money Concepts, the pattern marks the structural transition between an ongoing trend and an emerging reversal. Unlike a standard Break of Structure (BOS), which represents trend continuation, a CHoCH represents an initial structural shift that halts the prevailing order flow.

Why This Edge Works
Smart money players—commercial banks, sovereign funds, and market makers—cannot enter multi-billion-dollar positions at market price without causing severe slippage. To build a massive long position during an established downtrend, they must engineer liquidity by driving price below obvious swing lows. This triggers retail stop-loss sell orders and entices breakout sellers to flood the market with market sell orders.
Institutional algorithms absorb these sell orders with limit buy orders. Once they accumulate their inventory, they aggressively push price higher, violating the nearest lower high. When price breaks that swing high with displacement, retail traders who were short are forced to cover, while trend-following algorithms flip direction. The CHoCH confirms this institutional inventory shift. When price subsequently retraces into the demand zone or
order block
created during the expansion, institutional desks protect their average entry price, providing retail SMC traders with an asymmetric risk-to-reward entry.
The Setup Rules
Identify Higher Timeframe (HTF) Narrative:
Locate a key HTF point of interest (POI)—such as a 4-hour or Daily unmitigated supply/demand zone or major external liquidity pool (previous day high/low).
Wait for External Liquidity Run:
On your lower execution timeframe (15-minute or 5-minute), price must sweep liquidity above an old high (for bearish setups) or below an old low (for bullish setups).
Identify the Last Structural Pivot:
Mark the exact swing high that produced the lowest low in a downtrend (or the swing low that produced the highest high in an uptrend).
Demand Displacement Across the Pivot:
The break past this structural pivot must occur with strong momentum candles containing
Fair Value Gaps
(FVGs), not slow, grinding price action.
Require Full Candle Body Closes:
The structural break must close past the pivot level with the candle body. A simple wick through the level is classified as a liquidity grab, not a valid CHoCH.
Map the Discount/Premium Array:
Draw a
Fibonacci retracement
from the low of the sweep to the displacement high. Valid entry zones must sit in the Discount zone (below the 50% level) for buys, or the Premium zone (above 50%) for sells.

Entry Trigger
For a bullish setup, set a limit order at the proximal boundary of the unmitigated Order Block or
Fair Value Gap
inside the Discount zone after the candle body closes above the CHoCH pivot. Alternatively, wait for price to retrace into the zone and enter with a market order as soon as a 1-minute or 3-minute
bullish engulfing
candle or micro-CHoCH confirms rejection.
For a bearish setup, set a limit order at the proximal boundary of the unmitigated bearish Order Block or Fair Value Gap inside the Premium zone after a candle body closes below the CHoCH pivot.
Stop Loss & Profit Target
Place your stop loss precisely 2 to 5 pips beyond the extreme pivot low (or high) that swept liquidity prior to the CHoCH. Do not place stops arbitrarily inside the Order Block; if institutional order flow is genuinely bullish, the absolute low of the manipulation leg must remain protected.
Target external opposing liquidity pools on your trading timeframe. Target 1 sits at the internal swing high formed by the displacement leg, where you take off 50% of the position and move stops to breakeven. Target 2 sits at the higher-timeframe swing high or opposing major liquidity pool. A valid SMC CHoCH trade routinely delivers between 1:3 and 1:6 risk-to-reward ratios.
Trade Walkthrough: What It Looks Like on a Chart
Let us walk through a live execution example on EUR/USD using the 15-minute timeframe. The higher timeframe 4-hour trend has touched a major daily demand zone at 1.0820, putting us on high alert for a bullish reversal.
On the 15-minute chart, EUR/USD prints lower highs and lower lows. Price drops swiftly below 1.0825 to hit 1.0820, sweeping retail stop losses. Rather than continuing downward, price violently prints two large green candles totaling 70 pips. This move breaches the prior lower high sitting at 1.0865, closing decisively at 1.0890. This candle close at 1.0865 confirms the 15-minute Bullish CHoCH.
Between 1.0835 and 1.0845, the displacement leg left behind a clean 15-minute Bullish Order Block overlapping with an unmitigated Fair Value Gap. We measure the swing from 1.0820 to 1.0890; the 1.0835–1.0845 area rests deep inside the 62%–79% Discount zone.
As you can see in the chart above, we place a buy limit order at 1.0842 with our stop loss placed at 1.0818 (24 pips risk, giving 2 pips clearance under the 1.0820 sweep low). Price grinds down into London afternoon trade, taps 1.0840, fills our limit order, and immediately rejects higher.
We take partial profits at 1.0890 (Target 1, banking 48 pips or 2R) and trail our remaining position to the next significant buy-side liquidity pool at 1.0938. The trade reaches Target 2 for a total gain of 96 pips on the runner, locking in a blended 1:4 risk-to-reward ratio.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
Confusing Wick Sweeps with Real Structural Breaks:
If a candle merely pierces a swing pivot with an upper wick and closes back inside the range, it is an inducement or liquidity sweep, not a CHoCH. Entering prematurely here puts you right into smart money traps.
Trading Internal Structure as Major CHoCH:
Treating minor 1-minute fluctuations within a 15-minute consolidation as a true Change of Character leads to constant whipsaws. Only map the structural pivots that created the absolute swing extremes.
Ignoring Higher Timeframe Trend Bias:
A 5-minute CHoCH against a raging 4-hour institutional trend often results in a shallow retracement followed by aggressive trend continuation. Always align lower timeframe structural shifts with higher timeframe support and resistance zones.
Entering in Premium During Bullish Setups:
Chasing price when it breaks out above the CHoCH pivot destroys your risk-to-reward profile. If price does not pull back to discount zones or your defined order block, let the trade go.
Placing Stops Inside the Order Block:
Tucking stops tightly inside the body of an order block rather than beyond the liquidity sweep structural low exposes your position to secondary mitigations and spread expansion.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the difference between CHoCH and BOS in SMC?
A Change of Character (CHoCH) is the first break of structure against the prevailing trend, signaling an early reversal. A Break of Structure (BOS) is a structural break in the direction of the established trend, signaling continuation.
Which timeframe is best for trading a CHoCH setup?
The most reliable framework pairs a 4-hour or 1-hour timeframe for higher-level trend bias and POI identification with a 15-minute or 5-minute timeframe for the CHoCH confirmation and execution.
Can a CHoCH fail and resume the original trend?
Yes, a CHoCH fails when the break was merely a liquidity sweep on an unmapped higher timeframe or when it occurs in the middle of a consolidation range without higher timeframe backing.

Complete Guide to SMC Premium and Discount Zones in Forex
Ben Losier
October 19, 2026
ICT vs SMC
SMC premium and discount zones divide any active dealing range into two halves relative to a 50% equilibrium price, dictating that institutional algorithms accumulate long positions strictly at wholesale discount prices and distribute short positions at premium prices. If you are entering trades in the upper half of a dealing range expecting a bullish continuation, you are providing the exact exit liquidity institutional market makers require to close their books.

What Is Premium and Discount in SMC?
SMC premium and discount zones represent relative valuation bands established across an active price swing, formalizing the market auction principle that assets are expensive above equilibrium and cheap below it. The concept originated within Michael Huddleston’s Inner Circle Trader (ICT) curriculum as part of institutional pricing models and was subsequently integrated into modern
Smart Money Concepts
(SMC). The framework establishes that any market swing contains an absolute high (100%), an absolute low (0%), and an exact midpoint (50%) termed Equilibrium.

When price trades between 50% and 100% of the active range, it is in a Premium zone, where institutional algorithms look to sell to retail participants paying inflated prices. When price trades between 0% and 50%, it sits in a Discount zone, where institutional algorithms accumulate long orders at wholesale pricing. Using this model, smart money traders refuse to buy in premium or sell in discount, regardless of how strong a breakout candle appears.
Why This Edge Works
The mathematical and structural edge behind premium and discount zones rests on the mechanics of institutional liquidity delivery and the Interbank Price Delivery Algorithm (IPDA). Large institutions, central banks, and liquidity providers manage order sizes so massive that executing at market prices causes catastrophic slippage. To establish significant long inventory, they must buy where passive limit sell orders and aggressive stop-loss sell orders cluster: beneath swing lows and deep within the lower half of an established trading range.
Retail traders consistently fall victim to momentum chasing. When price aggressively expands upward, retail market participants buy at the top of the move out of Fear Of Missing Out (FOMO). That late surge of retail buy orders enters precisely in the premium zone, giving institutional algorithms the counterparty volume needed to offload long positions or open heavy short orders. By measuring the dealing range and waiting for price to rebalance back into discount territory before buying, you align your execution with institutional inventory accumulation rather than retail emotional reaction.
The Setup Rules
Identify the Dealing Range:
Locate the most recent major structural Break of Structure (BOS) or Change of Character (CHOCH) on your anchor timeframe (e.g., 4-Hour or 1-Hour). Define the absolute swing low that started the displacement and the absolute swing high where the move terminated.
Plot the Equilibrium:
Apply a
Fibonacci retracement
tool configured strictly with three coordinates: 0.0, 0.50, and 1.0. Anchor the tool from the swing low to the swing high for an uptrend, or from the swing high to the swing low for a downtrend. The 0.50 level marks your baseline equilibrium line.
Filter Directional Bias by Zone:
If your higher timeframe bias is bullish, your execution zone exists strictly beneath the 0.50 equilibrium line (0.0 to 0.499). Disqualify any long setup forming above the 50% level. If your bias is bearish, your execution zone exists strictly above the 0.50 line (0.501 to 1.0).
Identify High-Probability PD Arrays:
Inside the discount zone (for longs) or premium zone (for shorts), locate an unmitigated Point of Interest (POI). This must be a clean
Order Block
(OB), a
Fair Value Gap
(FVG), or a distinct pocket of sell-side liquidity waiting to be swept.
Wait for Retracement:
Allow price to retrace cleanly from the range extreme back across the 0.50 equilibrium level into your designated institutional array. Never enter blindly on the initial touch of the 50% line.

Entry Trigger
Once price enters your target discount or premium array, switch to an execution timeframe (typically the 1-Minute to 15-Minute chart depending on your higher timeframe anchor) to observe institutional order flow shifts.
Bullish Entry Trigger:
Wait for price to tap the discount array, sweep internal liquidity, and produce a clean lower-timeframe Change of Character (CHOCH) with an energetic displacement candle closing above the recent swing high. Enter on the subsequent limit retracement into the newly formed lower-timeframe Fair Value Gap or Bullish Order Block.
Bearish Entry Trigger:
Wait for price to tap the premium array, run buy-side liquidity, and print a lower-timeframe CHOCH with a decisive candle closing below the recent swing low. Enter via limit order on the retest of the resulting lower-timeframe imbalance.
Stop Loss & Profit Target
Stop loss placement in premium and discount trading is absolute: your stop must be anchored to structural invalidation, never an arbitrary dollar amount or fixed pip count.
Stop Loss Placement:
For long positions initiated inside a discount zone, position the stop loss 2 to 5 pips below the structural swing low that produced the lower-timeframe CHOCH, or directly below the extreme origin of the higher-timeframe dealing range low. If price breaches that extreme, the dealing range is invalid, and your trade hypothesis is dead. For shorts, position the stop 2 to 5 pips above the swing high.
Profit Target Placement:
Target liquidity pools on the opposing side of the range. For a long trade, take partial profits at the 50% equilibrium level (where smart money begins taking liquidity), and place your final target at the external Buy-Side Liquidity (BSL) sitting above the dealing range swing high.
Risk/Reward Ratio:
Because this strategy enters deep within the extremes of a dealing range with stops tightened by lower-timeframe structural shifts, setups routinely yield a minimum of 3:1 to 5:1 reward-to-risk.
Trade Walkthrough: What It Looks Like on a Chart
Consider a practical execution on EUR/USD on the 1-Hour chart. Following a decisive London session breakout, the pair prints a clean Break of Structure to the upside, establishing an active dealing range between a swing low at 1.0800 and an expansion high at 1.0950.
As you can see in the chart above, calculating the 50% equilibrium between 1.0800 and 1.0950 gives an exact midpoint of 1.0875. The space between 1.0875 and 1.0950 constitutes the Premium zone; the area between 1.0800 and 1.0875 represents the Discount zone. An unmitigated 1-Hour Bullish
Order Block
paired with an unfilled Fair Value Gap rests at 1.0830, well inside the discount half.
During the New York session, price begins a three-wave corrective pullback. Impatient retail buyers attempt to buy support bounces at 1.0900 and 1.0880, but these positions fail because price remains in premium pricing. The algorithms continue to discount the asset until price dips below 1.0875, plunging straight into the 1.0830 Order Block.
Dropping to the 5-Minute timeframe, price taps 1.0830, consolidates briefly, sweeps a micro-low at 1.0825, and aggressively displaces upward with a candle closing over 1.0845 (CHOCH). You enter a long position at 1.0835 on the retest of the micro FVG. Your stop loss is set at 1.0795 (5 pips below the dealing range origin low), giving 40 pips of risk. Your primary target is the external range high at 1.0955. The trade hits target during the New York afternoon, capturing 120 pips for a pristine 3:1 reward-to-risk outcome.

Common Mistakes to Avoid
Anchoring the Range to Minor Internal Pullbacks:
Traders frequently anchor their 50% tool to minor intraday fluctuations rather than clear structural highs and lows. If the swing high or swing low did not cause a legitimate structural break, your equilibrium reading is meaningless noise.
Buying in Premium During Strong Trends:
When the market prints large green candles, traders assume momentum will carry price indefinitely and buy well above the 50% line. Institutional algorithms regularly initiate deep pullbacks to extract liquidity, sweeping these premature long orders.
Treating the 50% Line as an Execution Level:
The equilibrium line is an analytical border, not an entry trigger. Placing blind limit orders directly on the 0.50 line without an underlying structural array (such as an Order Block or FVG) leads to frequent stopped-out trades.
Trading Without Multi-Timeframe Alignment:
Entering on a 5-minute discount zone while the 4-hour chart is deep inside an unmitigated premium supply zone invites disaster. Higher-timeframe order flow always overrules lower-timeframe ranges.

Frequently Asked Questions
What is the difference between Premium and Discount in SMC versus traditional Fibonacci retracements?
Traditional technical analysis often looks at arbitrary Fibonacci ratios like 38.2% or 61.8% in isolation. SMC uses the 50% line strictly as an auction market filter: anything above 50% is mathematically premium, and anything below is discount. Fibonacci levels like 62%, 70.5%, and 79% are only applied within the discount or premium zone as Optimal Trade Entry (OTE) markers.
Can you trade premium and discount zones on any timeframe?
Yes, the market is fractal, meaning premium and discount zones operate identically on a 1-minute chart as they do on a monthly chart. However, higher-timeframe zones (Daily, 4-Hour) govern broader liquidity cycles and hold far higher predictive reliability than lower-timeframe intraday ranges.
What should I do if price reverses before reaching the 50% equilibrium level?
If price fails to pull back into discount (for a bull market) and instead breaks the swing high, let it go. Chasing shallow pullbacks leads to buying at the top of structural ranges. Wait for a new structural dealing range to form, re-plot your equilibrium line, and wait patiently for institutional wholesale pricing.

Day 14: ICT PD Arrays vs SMC POIs — The Full Comparison
Micheal U.
April 10, 2026
All Trading Strategies
,
ICT vs SMC
ICT VS SMC
Closing Out Week Two. The Comparison That Ties Everything Together.
Welcome back. You’ve made it through fourteen days and two complete weeks of this series. You now understand market structure, liquidity, order blocks, FVGs, BOS and CHoCH, inducement, kill zones, multi-timeframe analysis, supply and demand zones, breaker blocks, mitigation blocks, OTE, and Fibonacci.
Today we close out Week 2 with the concept that unifies how both ICT and SMC organise all of those tools into an actionable trading framework.
ICT calls it the PD Array Matrix.
SMC calls it Points of Interest.
They are not identical — but they solve the same problem. And understanding both will let you move fluently between either methodology without confusion.
The Problem Both Systems Are Solving
You’ve spent days learning individual tools — order blocks, FVGs, breaker blocks. But when you sit down at a live chart, the question isn’t “what is an order block?” The question is:
“Of all the zones I can see on this chart right now, which one do I actually trade from?”
That is the problem PD Arrays and POIs both solve. They are filtering systems — ways of organising, ranking, and selecting the zones that matter most so you don’t waste time on the ones that don’t.
ICT PD Arrays — Premium and Discount Arrangement
The ICT PD Array — which stands for
Premium and Discount Array
— is a comprehensive map of all institutional reference points on a chart, organised by whether they sit in a premium or discount zone relative to the current dealing range.
The name tells you everything:
P
= Premium (price is expensive, above the 50% equilibrium — sell here),
D
= Discount (price is cheap, below equilibrium — buy here),
A
= Array (the entire collection of tools and levels within that range).
Every ICT concept you’ve learned is a component of the PD Array. When Huddleston says “look for a PD array in the discount zone,” he means: scan the lower half of the dealing range and find whichever of these tools is present — an order block, an FVG, a breaker block, a mitigation block, a rejection block, a liquidity void, an inversion FVG. The first one price reaches — starting from the current price and working toward the discount — is your target zone.
The full PD Array component list, organised by priority (bullish setup, discount zone):
PD Array Component
Description
Old lows / equal lows
External liquidity pool targets
Fair Value Gap (FVG)
Price imbalance — high probability fill zone
Order Block (OB)
Last opposing candle before impulse
Breaker Block
Failed OB after liquidity sweep — flipped
Mitigation Block
Failed OB without sweep — still valid zone
Rejection Block
Wick-dominant reversal zone
Volume Imbalance
Gap between candle bodies — internal inefficiency
Inversion FVG (IFVG)
Failed FVG now acting from opposite side
In a bearish setup, the same list applies in the premium zone — above the 50% equilibrium.
The key rule:
buy from discount PD arrays, sell from premium PD arrays.
Never enter a long from a premium zone or a short from a discount zone, regardless of how clean the individual setup looks.
SMC Points of Interest — The Same Logic, Simpler Language
The SMC community uses the term
Point of Interest (POI)
to describe any price zone where institutional activity is expected to have left an unfilled order — and where a meaningful market reaction is anticipated when price returns.
In practical terms, an SMC POI is almost always one of the following: an order block, a fair value gap, a breaker block, or a supply and demand zone. The difference from ICT’s PD Array framework is not in the tools themselves — it’s in how they’re selected and prioritised.
Where ICT uses a strict hierarchical matrix tied to the premium/discount framework, SMC uses a set of four quality criteria to decide whether a POI is worth trading:
1. It must have triggered a market structure shift or BOS.
A zone that produced an impulsive move confirming trend continuation or reversal is more significant than one that merely slowed price.
2. It must be protected by liquidity.
A strong POI usually has liquidity — equal highs, equal lows, or stop-loss clusters — resting just beyond it. That liquidity gives institutions a reason to defend the zone.
3. It must be unmitigated.
A POI that has already been revisited and fully tested has fewer unfilled orders remaining. Fresh, untouched zones react more reliably.
4. It must be the closest to current price.
Of all valid POIs on the chart, the one price encounters first as it retraces is the most immediately relevant.
The Shared Toolkit — Where Both Systems Agree
Despite the different names and organisational logic, both PD Arrays and POIs draw from the same underlying set of tools. Whether you call it a “bullish PD array in the discount zone” or a “bullish POI,” you’re describing the same thing: an order block or FVG sitting in a structurally significant location that price is likely to react from when it returns.
The tools are identical. The filters are different.
ICT filters by
where
the tool sits (premium or discount) AND by
when
price reaches it (Kill Zone).
SMC filters by
quality criteria
— structure shift, liquidity protection, freshness, and proximity — without enforcing a strict time window.
A Practical Example — Reading the Same Chart Both Ways
Imagine EURUSD is in a bullish daily trend. Price has made a new high and is now pulling back. You open the chart and see:
ICT approach:
Draw the Fibonacci from the swing low to the swing high. Mark the 50% equilibrium. Scan the discount zone (below 50%) for the first PD array component. You find a bullish order block at 67% retracement inside the discount zone. You check the time — it’s 2:30am EST, London Kill Zone is open. You mark this as your target zone and wait for price to reach it.
SMC approach:
You scan the pullback and identify a bullish order block that: (a) caused a BOS when price originally launched from it, (b) has sell-side liquidity resting just below it, (c) has never been retested, and (d) is the first zone price will encounter on the current pullback. It meets all four quality criteria. You mark it as your POI and wait.
Both traders are watching the same zone. One called it a discount-zone PD array. The other called it a high-probability POI. The zone is identical. The analysis process is slightly different. The trade — if it sets up — is the same.
The Key Difference in One Line
ICT:
“Is this tool in the correct premium or discount zone, and is it a Kill Zone?”
SMC:
“Does this zone meet all four quality criteria — structure, liquidity, freshness, and proximity?”
ICT adds time as a mandatory variable. SMC makes time optional but emphasises structural quality more explicitly. Advanced traders often apply both filters simultaneously — looking for a high-quality POI that also sits in the correct premium/discount zone AND forms during a Kill Zone. That triple confluence is where the highest-probability trades live.
